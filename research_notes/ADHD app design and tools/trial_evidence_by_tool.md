## 1. What are the components of the CBT / metacognitive therapy protocols for adult ADHD, which could be phone tools, and is there component-level evidence?

### Takeaway
The trialled protocols are multi-week, therapist-delivered packages built mostly from organisation, planning and time-management skills plus distractibility management and adaptive thinking; the packages have RCT support, the individual skills do not. No dismantling trial was found. The only component-level evidence located is one component network meta-analysis (Hamatani 2024), which points at organisational strategies, third-wave components and problem-solving as the likely active ingredients, by between-trial inference rather than by experiment.

### Cited Findings

**The protocols and their trials**

- Safren's programme ("Mastering your adult ADHD") has three core modules recommended for all clients — (1) psychoeducation and organising/planning, (2) coping with distractibility, (3) adaptive thinking — and two optional modules, procrastination and involvement of a partner. The first module typically runs four sessions; the distractibility module includes gauging attention span and "distractibility delay"; the adaptive-thinking module opens with a cognitive model of ADHD. [secondary] — [CHADD summary of the programme](https://chadd.org/attention-article/mastering-your-adult-adhd/); [Oxford therapist guide](https://academic.oup.com/book/1068)
- Safren et al. 2010, *JAMA*: 86 adults with clinically significant symptoms despite stable medication, CBT versus time-matched relaxation with educational support. CBT gave lower post-treatment scores on both independent-assessor-rated global severity and ADHD symptom ratings. An earlier trial compared CBT with continued medication alone in 31 adults. Outcome assessors were independent of treatment. [secondary; effect sizes not retrieved] — [doi:10.1001/jama.2010.1192](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2010.1192) (the DOI recorded as checked in `docs/02-evidence-rubric.md`)
- Safren has said that organisational and planning skills usually have to be in place before the other CBT interventions. This is clinical observation, not a dismantling result. [secondary] — [Psychology Tools interview](https://www.psychologytools.com/articles/meet-the-expert-dr-steven-safren)
- Solanto et al. 2010, *American Journal of Psychiatry*: 88 adults randomised to a 12-week manualised meta-cognitive therapy group (two-hour sessions, targeting time management, organisation and planning) or a supportive-therapy group matched for non-specific factors that deliberately avoided those strategies. A blind independent evaluator assessed response. Odds ratio for response 5.41 (95% CI 1.77 to 16.55), controlling for baseline severity. [abstract] — [PubMed 20231319](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20231319/); [doi:10.1176/appi.ajp.2009.09081123](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/abs/10.1176/appi.ajp.2009.09081123)
- Press coverage of the same trial gives 53% responders with meta-cognitive therapy against 28% with supportive therapy, response defined as at least a 30% reduction in inattention. [secondary] — press coverage of the release, for example [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/755099); which outlet carried the percentages was not pinned down. Dropout was 11% and 12%. [abstract-level table] — [Cochrane review table](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6494390/table/CD010840-tblf-0012)
- Young-Bramham / R&R2ADHD: Emilsson et al. 2011, *BMC Psychiatry*, randomised 54 medicated adults to CBT plus medication or treatment as usual plus medication and reported medium to large effects on ADHD symptoms that grew at three-month follow-up. A later report (Young et al., *Eur Arch Psychiatry Clin Neurosci*, published 2016/2017) covered functional outcomes in 95 medicated adults. The registry lists masking; who was masked was not confirmed. [abstract] — [PMC3155481](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3155481); [doi:10.1007/s00406-016-0735-0](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s00406-016-0735-0)
- Hesslinger / DBT-based structured skills group: the Swedish group describe their programme as DBT-based skills training "originally developed in Germany (Hesslinger, Richter & Philipsen, 2004)". Hirvikoski et al. 2011, *Behaviour Research and Therapy*: 51 adults, skills group versus a loosely structured discussion group; a significant reduction in ADHD symptoms in the skills group among completers who stayed stable on medication, none in controls, and no change in comorbidity in either. [abstract] — [Hirvikoski 2011 record](https://anhoriga.se/Artiklar-och-rapporter/2011/reduced-adhd-symptoms-in-adults-with-adhd-after-structured-skills-training-group-results-from-a-randomized-controlled-trial); [Karolinska programme page](https://ki.se/en/kind/interventions-for-adults-with-adhd-and-their-significant-others)
- Philipsen et al. 2015, *JAMA Psychiatry* 72(12):1199-1210 (COMPAS): seven German centres, group psychotherapy versus individual clinical management, crossed with methylphenidate versus placebo. At three months the observer-rated ADHD index went from 20.6 at baseline to adjusted means of 17.6 (group therapy) and 16.5 (clinical management), no significant difference: the structured group did not outperform non-specific individual management on the primary outcome. Effects were stable at one year. [abstract] — [News-Medical report of the paper](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20151109/Medication-not-psychotherapy-intensity-sways-treatment-outcome-in-adult-ADHD.aspx)
- A COMPAS follow-up on patients' own ratings found ADHD-specific group therapy rated better than non-specific management after treatment (z = 4.88) and at follow-up (z = 2.90). [abstract] — [library record](https://heibib.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/search/Record/1576197875)
- Halmøy et al. 2022, *BMC Psychiatry*: 121 adults from seven Norwegian clinics, 14 weeks of DBT-based group treatment versus treatment as usual, 104 (86%) completed. Larger mean reduction on the BRIEF-A (a self-report executive-function inventory): −12.8 versus −0.37, P = 0.005, effect size 0.64; also better on core symptoms and quality of life, sustained at six months. No significant difference in emotion-regulation change (DERS) at 14 weeks. 63% were on ADHD medication. [abstract / secondary] — [PubMed 36443712](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36443712/); [summary](https://www.physiciansweekly.com/the-effectiveness-of-dbt-bgt-compared-to-treatment-as-usual-for-adhd-adults)

**The packages, pooled**

- Lopez et al. 2018, Cochrane review: 14 RCTs, 700 adults. CBT versus other specific interventions: SMD 0.58 on clinician-rated symptoms (2 studies, 97 participants) and 0.44 on self-rated symptoms (4 studies, 156 participants), both low-quality evidence. Versus waiting list: self-rated depression SMD −0.36, anxiety −0.45. Mirrors of the abstract disagree on the sign convention. [abstract] — [PubMed 29566425](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29566425/); [Cochrane Library](https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD010840.pub2/information)
- Young, Moghaddam and Tickle, *Journal of Attention Disorders* (online 2016, print 2020): nine RCTs. Versus waiting list (4 trials, N = 160) SMD 0.76; versus active controls (3 trials, N = 191) SMD 0.43, 95% CI 0.14 to 0.71, p = .004. [abstract] — [PubMed 27554190](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27554190/)
- Knouse, Teller and Brooks 2017: 32 studies, up to 896 participants; g = 0.65 for symptoms versus control, smaller in studies with active controls. Includes uncontrolled pre-post data. [abstract] — [PubMed 28504540](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28504540/)

**Component-level evidence**

- Hamatani et al. 2024, *BMJ Mental Health* 27(1), systematic review and component network meta-analysis: 43 RCTs, 3,817 participants, search to 31 March 2022, PROSPERO CRD42022323898. For treatment response versus placebo: third-wave therapy OR 4.80 (95% CrI 2.50 to 9.10), behaviour therapy OR 3.50 (1.70 to 7.30), CBT OR 3.10 (1.70 to 5.70). At component level, organisational strategies (incremental OR 2.03, 95% CI 1.27 to 3.24) and third-wave components (incremental OR 1.95, 1.30 to 2.93) were associated with higher response, and problem-solving techniques with lower inattention (incremental SMD 0.42, 0.01 to 0.83). The abstract names those three as the likely contents of an optimal package. [abstract] — [PMC11683884](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11683884/); [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/62adc8d73fba45dbaab8385301515e5c)

### Inferences
- Every skill named in the brief (one list and calendar, task breakdown, prioritising, time estimation, distractibility delay, scheduling, procrastination, adaptive thinking) is a part of a package that meets Tier A. None has been tested alone. As phone tools they are Tier C by the rubric's own wording: a direct implementation of a Tier A component, itself untested.
- Organisation and planning have the best claim to being active ingredients: they are the whole of Solanto's protocol, the first and longest module of Safren's, the component Safren says must come first, and the component with the clearest signal in Hamatani. Adaptive thinking (cognitive restructuring) has no comparable signal in what was retrieved.
- Blinding matters and cuts both ways. Safren 2010 and Solanto 2010 both beat credible active controls on blinded assessment. COMPAS, the largest trial, found a structured skills group no better than non-specific clinical management on the blinded primary outcome while patients themselves rated it better. The honest summary is that structured skills treatment beats waiting lists clearly, beats active controls modestly (SMD about 0.4), and the size of the specific effect is uncertain.

### Gaps
- **No dismantling or additive-component RCT** isolating the organising, distractibility or adaptive-thinking modules turned up in the searches run. That is two targeted queries, not a systematic search, so absence is likely but not established.
- **Session-level contents of the manuals were not retrieved.** From recall, not confirmed this session: Safren's organising and planning module teaches one calendar and one task list used daily, breaking large tasks into steps, A/B/C priority ratings, problem-solving with an action plan, and a system for mail and papers; the distractibility module adds timed work matched to measured attention span, writing distractions down to return to later (the "delay"), reminders and alarms, and changing the work environment. Solanto's programme adds time-awareness exercises including estimating how long tasks take. Confirm against the Oxford therapist guide and Solanto's manual before attributing a tool to a named component.
- **Whether Hamatani 2024 is adults-only was not confirmed** (the query was cut by the search budget). If it pools children and adults, its component findings are weaker support for adult tools. Check the eligibility criteria at PMC11683884.
- Safren 2010's numbers were not retrieved. Recall only: about a 4.6-point difference on the ADHD Rating Scale and roughly twice the responder rate. Take them from the paper.
- A summary returned by the search described the COMPAS group as "cognitive behavioural, not DBT-based". My understanding is that it used the Hesslinger DBT-based skills manual. Unresolved; check the paper's methods.
- Knouse et al.'s work on whether skill use mediates CBT outcome was not retrieved (budget). Knouse 2017's blinded-rater subgroup figure was not confirmed.
- Other package trials named in older reviews (Virta, Weiss, Dittner, Cherkasova, van Emmerik-van Oortmerssen) were not searched individually.

## 2. What is the evidence for digital, app-delivered or internet-delivered CBT and self-guided interventions for adult ADHD?

### Takeaway
There are now at least six RCTs of digital skills programmes in adults, including one large trial (attexis, n = 337, d = 0.85) and one with an active control (MyADHD, n = 120, d = 0.70). All report benefit, and all rely on unblinded self-report, mostly against waiting list or usual care, several with commercial interests. That is real trial evidence at high risk of bias: Tier B, not A. No trial tests a single tool in isolation.

### Cited Findings
- **attexis (READ-ADHD)**, D'Amelio et al., *Psychological Medicine*, March 2026: 337 adults with confirmed ADHD randomised 1:1 to a self-guided CBT-based digital programme plus usual care or usual care alone. Preprint figures: baseline-adjusted mean difference −5.0 points on the Adult ADHD Self-Report Scale at three months, d = 0.85 (intention to treat). Attrition at three months 9.1% versus 3.5%. All outcomes were self-reported, so no blinded assessors. Registered NCT06221930. The manufacturer's material also reports gains in work and social functioning, self-esteem, quality of life and depressive symptoms. The product was added to Germany's DiGA directory in August 2025. [abstract of preprint; published figures not checked] — [journal page](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/psychological-medicine/article/effectiveness-of-attexis-a-digital-intervention-based-on-cognitive-behavioral-therapy-for-adults-with-adhd-a-randomized-controlled-trial/BBB55FF99ADF58005B1BD9B00AD0AF96); [medRxiv preprint](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.08.27.25334574.full.pdf); [university release](https://www.uni-saarland.de/en/news/clinical-study-shows-digital-intervention-can-reduce-adhd-symptoms-in-adults-44662.html)
- The attexis authors count five earlier small RCTs of digital interventions for core symptoms, with effect sizes between d = 0.42 and 1.21. [abstract of preprint] — [medRxiv preprint](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.08.27.25334574.full.pdf)
- **MyADHD**, Kenter et al. 2023, *Internet Interventions*: 120 adults with a self-reported diagnosis randomised to a self-guided internet intervention or online psychoeducation. Between-group d = 0.70 on self-reported ADHD symptoms and d = 0.53 on quality of life, maintained at three months; 45.0% versus just under 11% reported a clinically meaningful decline. Recruited online, so not necessarily representative. Registered NCT04726813. [abstract] — [PMC10033990](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10033990); [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/2aa213698a7543d9be32d2b1823bb6ad)
- **Inflow**, Antshel, McBride and Knouse, *Journal of Attention Disorders* (doi:10.1177/10870547251384462; indexed 2025, described elsewhere as 2026): 154 adults aged 18 to 55, eight weeks of a CBT-informed app versus waiting list. Significant effects on inattentive symptoms, hyperactive-impulsive symptoms and ADHD-related quality of life; none on functional impairment. Inattention gains tracked the number of exercises completed. The authors say confidence is limited by the waiting-list design. Declared interests include a paid consultancy with stock options and company co-founders among the authors; the company supplied access. [secondary; no numeric effect size retrieved] — [journal PDF link](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.1177/10870547251384462); [summary](https://www.simplypsychology.org/news/digital-therapy-bridges-the-adult-adhd-gap)
- Earlier Inflow evidence was an uncontrolled seven-week feasibility study (Knouse et al. 2022, *PLOS Digital Health*, 240 recruited) whose authors said an RCT was needed. [abstract] — [PMC9931323](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9931323/)
- **Nasri et al. 2023**, *Internet Interventions*: 104 adults randomised to internet CBT, internet applied relaxation, or treatment as usual. Both active arms beat usual care on the ASRS and held at 12 months; they did not differ from each other. Responders 25% in each active arm, 3% with usual care. [abstract] — [PMC10359875](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10359875)
- **Pettersson et al. 2017**, *Journal of Attention Disorders*: 45 adults, self-help internet CBT, the same with weekly group sessions, or waiting list. Self-help arm versus waiting list d = 1.07 at post-treatment (intention to treat); the arm with added group meetings was not significantly different from waiting list. [abstract / secondary] — [record](https://metatoc.com/papers/52986-internet-based-cognitive-behavioral-therapy-for-adults-with-adhd-in-outpatient-psychiatric-care-a-randomized-trial); [PMC8632851](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8632851)
- **Living Smart**, Moëll et al. 2015, *Internet Interventions*: 57 adults with ADHD or sub-clinical ADHD, a six-week online course with a coach teaching use of ordinary smartphone apps to organise daily life (n = 29) versus waiting list (n = 28). ASRS inattention fell from 28.1 (SD 4.5) to 22.9 (SD 4.3) in the course group, a significantly larger fall than in controls. [abstract] — [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/1b7369c5f7174fb7a75c47bdf1fceb47)
- Adherence is the known weak point of unguided delivery: one self-guided internet programme for adults with ADHD was not completed by 77% of participants, which prompted a trial of weekly SMS reminders (Nordby et al. 2022, 109 adults; result not retrieved). [abstract] — [PMC9149073](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9149073)
- Shou et al. 2022, *Frontiers in Psychology*, meta-analysis of online interventions: six RCTs, 261 patients, adults and children pooled, SMD −0.73 (95% CI −1.01 to −0.44) on attention, mostly against waiting list. [abstract] — [PMC9274127](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9274127/)
- A 2025 meta-analysis of digital interventions in *Computers in Human Behavior Reports*: 23 RCTs, 1,472 participants, g = −0.32 on overall symptoms, g = −0.25 on inattention, nothing significant on hyperactivity/impulsivity, and no significant benefit for telehealth or mobile-health formats. Whether the sample was adult-only was not stated in what was retrieved, and the link is the search result's, not confirmed as this paper. [secondary] — [repository record](https://ink.library.smu.edu.sg/soss_research/4200)
- An internet CBT trial with 200 participants against waiting list, six-month primary follow-up, is registered and ongoing. [registry] — [NCT05700539](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05700539)

### Inferences
- **Guided versus unguided.** No head-to-head trial of therapist-guided against unguided delivery was found. Indirectly, the fully self-guided programmes (attexis, MyADHD, Pettersson's self-help arm) did not do visibly worse than supported ones, and in Pettersson adding weekly groups did not help. Self-guided delivery looks viable for effect, with completion as the limit.
- **Non-specific effects are large.** In Nasri, applied relaxation matched CBT. In Kenter, the one trial with an active digital control, the effect was still d = 0.70, which is the best single argument that skills content adds something beyond attention and expectancy. Every outcome in every one of these trials is self-report by participants who knew their allocation.
- These are multi-week structured programmes with sequenced modules and exercises. Evidence for a programme does not transfer to a standalone timer, list or planner inside a different app.

### Gaps
- Numeric effect sizes, attrition and the full author list for the Inflow RCT were not retrieved.
- The attexis figures are from the preprint; the published paper may differ.
- A search summary quoted greater dropout from internet CBT than waiting list (RR 1.39, 95% CI 1.03 to 1.88; 8 studies, 585 participants) without a traceable source. A possible origin is a 2025 *Frontiers in Psychiatry* review (10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1564506). Not usable until located.
- Nasri 2023's between-group effect sizes, dropout and degree of therapist support were not retrieved (budget).
- No adult-only meta-analysis of digital CBT with dropout data was found.

## 3. What is the evidence in adults with ADHD for each named technique?

### Takeaway
Outside full CBT-type packages, only mindfulness, DBT-based skills groups, psychoeducation groups and digital CBT programmes have more than one adult RCT, and all sit at Tier B on bias or blinding grounds. For body doubling, visual timers, Pomodoro-style work, implementation intentions, journaling and habit tracking the honest answer is no direct trial evidence in adults with ADHD.

| Medication adherence tools | Text reminders, adherence apps | Text programme: 68% versus 34% adherent, but against historical controls ([report](https://www.consultantlive.com/view/higher-adhd-treatment-adherence-rates-linked-text-message-intervention)); app RCT, n = 73, no difference in medication possession ([PMC10377453](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10377453)) | Blinded in the app RCT | **B**, weak and mixed |
| Symptom and medication self-monitoring diary | A daily record taken to the prescriber | No trial found showing a diary changes outcomes | n/a | **A (supporting)** only by the "directly supports" limb, not by trial |

#### Detail behind the table

- **Mindfulness.** Kim and Jung 2025, *Medicine* (doi:10.1097/MD.0000000000044308, published 12 September 2025), meta-analysed RCTs in which mindfulness was central; its abstract says effects on emotional wellbeing and mindfulness skills remain inconclusive. A secondary source attributes to it SMDs of 0.48 (self-reported symptoms), 0.32 (observer-rated) and 0.56 (everyday functioning) across ten studies, and no significant effect on negative affect; a different summary page claims 12 RCTs and reductions in anxiety and depression. The two conflict and neither was checked. [abstract / secondary] — [PMC12440486](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12440486)
- Janssen et al. 2019, *Psychological Medicine* 49(1):55-65: multicentre, single-blind, 60 adults to MBCT plus usual care and 60 to usual care; primary outcome was blinded clinician-rated symptoms, d = 0.41 per a later paper; self-reported symptoms also improved and held at six months. [abstract / secondary] — [doi:10.1017/S0033291718000429](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0033291718000429); [review summary](https://www.mdedge.com/psychiatry/article/265590/adhd/adult-adhd-6-studies-nonpharmacologic-interventions/page/0/2)
- **Exercise.** Xu, Zhao and Hu 2026, *Psychology of Sport & Exercise*: four studies, 170 adults, small but consistent improvement in core symptoms after single sessions; authors call it preliminary. [secondary] — [summary](https://adhdevidence.org/blog/meta-analysis-finds-small-to-moderate-benefits-of-single-exercise-sessions-for-adult-adhd)
- A 2025 *Journal of Global Health* meta-analysis of RCTs (8 articles, 14 studies, 373 participants) reports inhibitory-control effects of SMD −0.65 for acute and −1.77 for chronic exercise, with few long-term RCTs and high heterogeneity. Adult-only status not confirmed. [abstract] — [jogh-15-04025](https://jogh.org/2025/jogh-15-04025)
- An unpublished 2022 master's-thesis meta-analysis (10 studies, N = 286) found g = 0.34 across outcomes, g = 0.55 for aerobic exercise. Not peer reviewed. [secondary] — [Murdoch repository](https://researchportal.murdoch.edu.au/esploro/outputs/graduate/Sweat-or-Rest-The-effects-of/991005548668507891)
- **Sleep.** Van Andel et al. (PhASE): 51 adults with ADHD and delayed sleep phase received sleep education plus three weeks of placebo, low-dose melatonin, or melatonin with morning bright light. Melatonin advanced circadian phase by 1 h 28 min and the combination by 1 h 58 min. ADHD symptoms fell 14% with melatonin alone and not in the other arms, and both phase and symptoms returned to baseline two weeks after stopping. A secondary analysis (49 participants, *Journal of Biological Rhythms* 2022) found the combination advanced phase by two hours without affecting ADHD symptoms. [secondary / abstract] — [APSARD summary](https://apsard.org/the-fase-study-best-treatment-of-delayed-sleep-phase-syndrome-in-adult-adhd/); [doi:10.1177/07487304221124659](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/07487304221124659)
- A 2021 review of sleep interventions in ADHD lists a CBT-I group programme for adults only as an open study with three-month follow-up and notes that many sleep interventions in use remain unstudied in ADHD. [abstract-level table] — [PMC8534229](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8534229)
- **Psychoeducation.** Hirvikoski et al. 2017, *European Psychiatry* (PEGASUS): pragmatic multicentre RCT, group psychoeducation for adults and their significant others added to usual care; knowledge about ADHD d = 0.97 (95% CI 0.61 to 1.31), also better life satisfaction; per-protocol analysis. [abstract] — [record](https://core-cms.cambridgecore.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/7499896E20CF58984C603053A386A71B/S0924933800069893a.pdf/psychoeducational-groups-for-adults-with-adhd-and-their-significant-others-pegasus-a-pragmatic-multicenter-and-randomized-controlled-trial.pdf)
- Selaskowski et al. 2022, *Psychiatry Research*: 60 adults in eight-week psychoeducation groups supported by an app (n = 30) or paper brochures (n = 30). Observer-rated symptoms fell in both; the app arm did better on inattention, impulsivity and homework completion. No untreated control. [abstract] — [record](https://hs-flensburg.de/publikationen/journal-article/1026)
- **Coaching.** Ahmann and Saviet's manualised protocol paper states that no research had explored individual coaching for adults with ADHD and that the two adult studies were of group coaching (Bloemen et al. 2007; Kubik 2010); other coaching studies are in college students. [secondary] — [protocol paper](https://radar.brookes.ac.uk/radar/items/a4a8d49e-0f1c-4741-b550-82533d403b7e/1)
- **Implementation intentions.** In 45 inpatient children (mean age 10.7) with ADHD, forming if-then plans improved delay of gratification (Gawrilow, Gollwitzer and Oettingen 2011). Adults with ADHD show planning and plan-initiation deficits relative to controls (Fuermaier and colleagues, 45 versus 45). No intervention study in adults with ADHD was found. [abstract / secondary] — [Gawrilow 2011](https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/sites/default/files/Gawrilow_Gollwitzer_Oettingen_Delay_2011-2.pdf); [summary of Fuermaier](https://www.goodtherapy.org/blog/adults-with-adhd-experience-difficulties-planning-future-0419131)
- **Goal management training.** In de Braek et al. 2017: a small pilot of training plus psychoeducation, benefit only on clinician-rated everyday cognitive functioning. An open study of 21 adults followed (*Frontiers in Psychology* 2021). Hanssen et al. 2023, an RCT of group training with psychoeducation and individual goal guidance versus usual care: both groups improved on perceived everyday executive functioning, the primary outcome, and the training group had a significantly larger fall in anxiety. [abstract] — [Maastricht record](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/goal-management-training-in-adults-with-adhd-an-intervention-stud); [open study](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.659480); [PubMed 38046113](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38046113)
- **Body doubling.** A 2025 preprint (12 adults, virtual reality, alone versus human double versus AI double) states that no peer-reviewed controlled trial had examined body doubling in adults with ADHD and notes one earlier study with ADHD participants that found no significant effect. A 2026 roadmap paper calls the topic new and under-researched. A survey of about 1,600 college students found modest benefit and that some students with ADHD may do worse. A 117-person survey reporting benefit was published by the company running the service. [preprint / secondary] — [arXiv 2509.12153](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.12153); [arXiv 2605.07851](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.07851); [Popular Science](https://www.popsci.com/?p=771951); [provider survey](https://neurodiversity.org/?p=11449)
- **Medication adherence.** A Massachusetts General Hospital text-messaging programme in 117 adults on stimulants: 68% adherent versus 34% with usual care (OR 4.04); the adult comparison used historical record controls, not concurrent randomisation, and was reported at a 2021 meeting. [secondary] — [report](https://www.consultantlive.com/view/higher-adhd-treatment-adherence-rates-linked-text-message-intervention)
- FOCUS ADHD app: randomised, blinded, 73 adults, usual care versus app versus app plus a medication discount; no significant difference in medication possession ratio. [abstract] — [PMC10377453](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10377453)

### Inferences
- The rubric's Tier C tools (timers, body doubling, implementation-intention prompts, task-breaking templates) are correctly placed: none has adult trial evidence. Body doubling and Pomodoro are not components of any retrieved Tier A protocol, so their C rests on mechanism alone, the weaker of the rubric's two C limbs. The one body-doubling data point with ADHD participants was null.
- Implementation intentions may be over-described if called a technique "used inside a Tier A protocol": they were not confirmed as a named component of Safren's or Solanto's manuals in what was retrieved. The child findings do not transfer.
- Sleep at Tier B rests entirely on general-population CBT-I evidence. No controlled trial of a behavioural sleep intervention in adults with ADHD was located.
- Exercise at Tier B is generous on current adult data, which is mostly single-session laboratory work. A wording such as "small, short-lived effects after a single session; little evidence on regular exercise" fits what was found.
- A medication diary is Tier A only by definition (it supports an established treatment). No trial shows that keeping one improves outcomes, and the single app RCT on adherence was null. Library wording should not imply the log itself has been tested.
- Habit tracking has neither evidence nor a protocol basis.

### Gaps
- Not searched before the budget ran out: journaling or expressive writing, habit tracking, time-perception or time-estimation training, organisational skills training in college students (for example the ACCESS programme), and prospective-memory aids. "Not found" for these means not looked for adequately.
- Visual timers: one combined query returned nothing for adults. Child studies of time-assistive devices exist from recall, were not retrieved, and would not transfer.
- General-population evidence for implementation intentions and for CBT-I was not retrieved; the B and C placements that depend on it are unreferenced here.
- Vidal 2013 (psychoeducation versus CBT), Hoxhaj 2018 (mindfulness versus psychoeducation) and Jernelöv 2019 (CBT-I pilot) were not retrieved.
- Whether a controlled trial of regular exercise in adults with ADHD has reported since 2023 was not established.
- Hanssen 2023's between-group result on its primary outcome needs reading in the paper; "both groups improved" suggests no advantage but was not confirmed. Whether In de Braek 2017 was randomised was reported inconsistently.

## 4. What has been tested and found ineffective or weak under blinded assessment, and has 2023 to 2026 changed the picture?

### Takeaway
Nothing since 2023 rehabilitates the excluded list. Neurofeedback now has a second large blinded-outcome meta-analysis showing essentially zero effect (SMD 0.04), and computerised cognitive training still shows no effect on overall symptoms when raters are blinded. Omega-3 remains non-significant overall.

### Cited Findings
- **Computerised cognitive training.** Westwood et al. 2023, *Molecular Psychiatry*: 36 RCTs, 17 of them working-memory training. With probably blinded outcomes, no effect on total ADHD symptoms or hyperactivity/impulsivity; a small effect on inattention (SMD 0.17) limited to the setting where training took place; working memory itself improved (verbal working memory: 15 RCTs, 753 participants, small to medium) with no transfer to other neuropsychological or academic outcomes. Findings held for working-memory training and semi-active controls. [abstract] — [PMC10208955](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10208955/); [PubMed 36977764](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36977764/)
- A 2023 adult-specific meta-analysis, "Computerized cognitive interventions for adults with ADHD", concluded that training has a small positive effect in adults. Which outcome carried the effect was not retrieved. [secondary] — [record](https://aminer.org/pub/640b0e2e90e50fcafd11eed8/computerized-cognitive-interventions-for-adults-with-adhd-a-systematic-review-and-meta)
- **Neurofeedback.** Westwood et al., *JAMA Psychiatry* 2025;82:118-129 (online 11 December 2024): 38 RCTs, 2,472 participants aged 5 to 40. Probably blinded symptom ratings: SMD 0.04 (95% CI −0.10 to 0.18; k = 20, n = 1,214). Similar with adults excluded. The only neuropsychological gain was processing speed (SMD 0.35, 0.01 to 0.69). A sensitivity analysis limited to standard protocols gave SMD 0.21. [abstract] — [PubMed 39661381](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39661381/); [journal abstract](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapsychiatry/article-abstract/2827733)
- The 2016 predecessor found effects only on ratings by people closest to treatment, not with probably blinded ratings or sham controls. [abstract] — [PubMed 27238063](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27238063/)
- In the 2025 digital-interventions meta-analysis, neurofeedback did worse than combination-based digital interventions. [secondary] — [repository record](https://ink.library.smu.edu.sg/soss_research/4200)
- **Supplements.** A 2023 meta-analysis in the *Journal of Clinical Psychiatry* (22 RCTs, 1,789 participants, mostly children): omega-3 did not significantly improve core symptoms overall (SMD −0.16, 95% CI −0.34 to 0.01, P = .07); in trials of at least four months, SMD −0.35 (−0.61 to −0.09). Neither EPA dose nor EPA:DHA ratio mattered. [abstract] — [PubMed 37656283](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37656283/)
- **Relaxation training** as a treatment was reported as worse than placebo on self-rated symptoms in the 2025 Lancet Psychiatry network meta-analysis. [secondary] — [summary](https://www.adhdevidence.org/blog/from-meds-to-mindfulness-what-actually-works-for-adult-adhd)

### Inferences
- The exclusion entries in `docs/02-evidence-rubric.md` stand, and the neurofeedback entry can now cite a 2025 blinded-outcome meta-analysis rather than "small or absent".
- There is a tension to state plainly: Ostinelli 2025 reports "cognitive remediation" as better than placebo (SMD −1.35, see section 5), while Westwood 2023 finds computerised training null on blinded outcomes. The Ostinelli estimate has a very wide interval and, on the evidence retrieved, applies to one rater type only. It is not grounds to revisit the exclusion, but a Library entry that ignores it will be challenged.
- The adult-only cognitive-training meta-analysis reporting a "small positive effect" needs reading before the exclusion entry says anything specific about adults.

### Gaps
- Schönenberg et al. 2017 (*Lancet Psychiatry*), the main adult neurofeedback trial, was not retrieved. From recall: neurofeedback was no better than sham or than group meta-cognitive therapy. Confirm.
- Adult micronutrient trials (for example Rucklidge 2014) and elimination diets in adults were not retrieved; no 2023 to 2026 adult evidence on either surfaced.
- A 2025 trial of a cognitive-training app in adults (NeuroNation MED) appeared in results by title only; its design and blinding are unknown. — candidate: psypost.org coverage, not read.
- Authors and venue of the adult cognitive-training meta-analysis were not confirmed (from recall: Elbe and colleagues, *Neuropsychology*).

## 5. What do the 2024 to 2026 network meta-analyses and umbrella reviews conclude?

### Takeaway
Four syntheses agree that CBT is the best-supported non-drug treatment for adults and that the evidence is short-term. They disagree on confidence: the Lancet Psychiatry network meta-analysis finds non-drug effects on clinician ratings but not on patients' own, the Frontiers analysis rates most evidence low or very low, and the BMJ umbrella review gives CBT at least moderate certainty.

### Cited Findings
- **Ostinelli et al. 2025**, *Lancet Psychiatry* 12(1):32-43, doi:10.1016/S2215-0366(24)00360-2, PMID 39701638. Component network meta-analysis of 113 RCTs, 14,887 adults, covering pharmacological, psychological and neurostimulatory treatments. [abstract] — [repository PDF](https://iris.unimo.it/retrieve/be4c8ea5-8571-4778-9ccb-43e6f90f019c/PIIS2215036624003602.pdf); [PubMed mirror](https://www.unboundmedicine.com/medline/citation/39701638/Comparative_efficacy_and_acceptability_of_pharmacological_psychological_and_neurostimulatory_interventions_for_ADHD_in_adults:_a_systematic_review_and_component_network_meta_analysis_)
- Its headline: at 12 weeks only stimulants and atomoxetine reduced core symptoms on both clinician-rated and self-rated scales. Non-drug interventions were inconsistent across raters; coverage describes them as effective when clinicians rated symptoms and not when patients did. Few trials ran past 26 weeks, and evidence on quality of life was thin. [secondary] — [Healio](https://cm.healio.com/news/psychiatry/20250110/only-stimulants-atomoxetine-reduce-core-adhd-symptoms-in-adults-metaanalysis-shows); [Psychiatric Times](https://www.psychiatrictimes.com/view/what-works-for-adults-with-adhd); [Science Media Centre](https://www.sciencemediacentre.org/expert-reaction-to-network-meta-analysis-of-pharmacological-psychological-and-neurostimulatory-interventions-for-adhd/)
- Abstract figures retrieved: cognitive behavioural therapy SMD −0.76 (95% CI −1.26 to −0.26); cognitive remediation −1.35 (−2.42 to −0.27). The excerpt did not say which rater these belong to. [abstract excerpt] — [Bern repository record](https://boris-portal.unibe.ch/handle/20.500.12422/194663)
- Published correspondence read the same data as showing benefits that are short-lived, and suggested rater discrepancy as one reason non-drug results look weak. [secondary] — [Lancet Psychiatry correspondence](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lanpsy/article/PIIS2215-0366%2825%2900045-8/fulltext)
- **Yang, Zhang, Yu and Wang 2025**, *Frontiers in Psychiatry*, doi:10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1516878, published 31 January 2025. Network meta-analysis of 37 RCTs, 2,289 participants, ten non-drug therapies; four databases to September 2024; trials of at least three weeks; PROSPERO CRD42024432912. CBT beat control on core symptoms in the short and the long term and on depression and anxiety. The authors conclude CBT may be the most effective option and mindfulness-based cognitive therapy a reasonable choice for people without comorbidities. Confidence was "very low" or "low" for most treatments and 48.6% of trials were at high risk of bias. [abstract] — [article](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1516878/full)
- **Gosling et al. 2025**, *BMJ*, doi:10.1136/bmj-2025-085875, published 26 November 2025. Umbrella review of 115 meta-analyses covering 221 population-intervention-comparator-outcome combinations, certainty rated with an adapted GRADE. In adults, atomoxetine, CBT and methylphenidate showed at least moderate certainty of efficacy on symptoms with medium effect sizes; mindfulness in adults showed a large effect size with low certainty. All findings are short-term. An accompanying platform, ebiadhd-database.org, gives effects by age, time point and outcome. [abstract] — [PubMed 41297970](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41297970/); [Oxford repository copy](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:b70e7628-55c6-47be-a95f-306cafb8506b/files/rn870zt01r)
- **Hamatani et al. 2024**, *BMJ Mental Health*: see section 1. Third-wave therapy, behaviour therapy and CBT all beat placebo on response; organisational strategies and third-wave components were the components with signal. [abstract] — [PMC11683884](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11683884/)
- **Westwood et al. 2025**, *JAMA Psychiatry* (neurofeedback): see section 4. [abstract] — [PubMed 39661381](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39661381/)

### Inferences
- The sentence in `docs/02-evidence-rubric.md` about Yang 2025 (37 RCTs, CBT most effective short and long term, low or very low confidence, roughly half of trials at high risk of bias) matches the abstract as retrieved.
- Ostinelli 2025 is the largest and most rigorous of these and is not in the rubric's citation list. Its rater finding is the single most important caveat for Tier A wording on planning tools: on patients' own ratings, which are the only ratings an app user has, non-drug treatments did not separate from placebo at 12 weeks.
- Gosling 2025 and Ostinelli 2025 can both be right: one grades the certainty that CBT has some effect, the other asks whether it shows on every rater. A Library entry should carry both.

### Gaps
- **Ostinelli's rater attribution and remaining figures were not confirmed.** From recall of the abstract, not verified this session: CBT, cognitive remediation, mindfulness (about −0.79), psychoeducation (about −0.77) and transcranial direct current stimulation (about −0.78) were better than placebo on clinician-rated measures only. The two figures the search did return match this recall exactly, which is reassuring and is not verification. Read the abstract at PMID 39701638.
- A search summary quoted "CBT SMD −2.50 (−4.61 to −0.39)" and "mindfulness-based intervention −3.42 (−6.57 to −0.27)" from a Frontiers paper without saying which. These look like raw mean differences and may be Yang 2025's; do not use until located.
- Yang 2025's per-treatment SMDs, rankings and the claim in the rubric that mindfulness-based cognitive therapy "ranked second" were not verified.
- Gosling 2025's adult effect sizes and exact certainty labels per outcome were not retrieved; the platform would give them.
- Ostinelli's acceptability and tolerability results for psychological treatments were not retrieved.

## 6. What do current guidelines recommend for non-drug support in adults?

### Takeaway
Guidelines consistently place structured psychological support, usually CBT-based and preceded by psychoeducation, as an adjunct to medication or as the option for adults who do not take it. None was found to recommend a specific app or digital tool. Guideline wording was retrieved through secondary summaries only.

### Cited Findings
- **NICE NG87** (2018, last updated 2019). Non-pharmacological treatment should be considered for adults who have chosen not to take medication, who have difficulty adhering to it, or who found it ineffective or could not tolerate it, and alongside medication when symptoms still cause impairment. The minimum is a structured supportive psychological intervention focused on ADHD. Medication is offered to adults when symptoms still cause significant impairment after environmental modifications have been implemented and reviewed. [secondary reproduction of the guideline] — [NG87 PDF copy](https://www.nursinginpractice.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/04/NG87_ADHD.pdf)
- The NIHR's summary adds that the guideline says non-pharmacological treatment may involve elements of, or a full course of, CBT. [secondary] — [NIHR Evidence alert](https://evidence.nihr.ac.uk/alert/cognitive-behavioural-therapy-could-benefit-adults-with-attention-deficit-hyperactivity-disorder-adhd/)
- **AADPA 2022** (Australia; endorsed by the NHMRC in July 2022). Cognitive-behavioural interventions for adolescents and adults: one professional summary says they "should be offered", a news report says "consider", so the strength of the recommendation is reported inconsistently. ADHD coaching "could be considered" as part of a wider plan, with few high-quality studies behind it. The guideline also covers sleep, diet and physical activity. Sources disagree on whether it has 113 or 132 recommendations. [secondary] — [Australian Psychological Society](https://psychology.org.au/for-members/Publications/inpsych/2022/Summer-2022/evidence-based-guidelines-for-adhd); [RACGP](https://www1.racgp.org.au/newsgp/clinical/new-australian-adhd-guidelines-released); [journal article on psychologists' role](https://jnzccp.scholasticahq.com/article/81053); [Deakin summary](https://lifespan.deakin.edu.au/2022/10/what-is-adhd-coaching-and-do-i-really-need-it/)
- **CADDRA** (Canada). A 2017 summary table lists psychoeducation (including strategy instruction and organisational skills), behavioural interventions (environmental management, ADHD coaching, lifestyle change), psychotherapy including CBT, and educational or vocational accommodations. Mindfulness did not appear in that table. [secondary, dated] — [summary table](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5567978/table/Tab2)
- **Updated European Consensus Statement** (Kooij et al. 2019, *European Psychiatry*, doi:10.1016/j.eurpsy.2018.11.001). Psychoeducation is the recommended first step after diagnosis; psychological therapy such as CBT should be available as an adjunct to medication in all adult ADHD services. [secondary] — [consensus statement](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/707E2A36539213CF85EACCA576F47427/core-reader)

### Inferences
- Guidelines back the category (structured, ADHD-focused psychological support; psychoeducation; environmental changes), not any individual tool. They support an app describing its planning tools as drawn from recommended treatment; they do not support saying a guideline recommends the tools.
- "Environmental modifications" in NICE is the closest guideline hook for timers, reminders and externalised structure, and NICE treats it as a reasonable adjustment rather than a treatment with an evidence grade.
- No guideline retrieved ranks coaching above "could be considered", which fits the absence of adult trials.

### Gaps
- **No guideline was read at source.** The primary texts are at nice.org.uk/guidance/ng87 and adhdguideline.aadpa.com.au; recommendation numbers and exact wording need checking there.
- Whether NICE has updated or scheduled an update to NG87 in 2024 to 2026, and what the NHS England ADHD taskforce concluded about non-drug support, was not established (the query was cut by the search budget).
- The current CADDRA edition and its position on mindfulness, coaching and digital tools were not retrieved.
- The German and other national guidelines were not searched.
