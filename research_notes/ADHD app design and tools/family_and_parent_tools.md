## 1. Behavioural parent training: active components, and blinded versus unblinded effects

### Takeaway
Parent training reliably improves parenting and child conduct problems, and those effects survive probably-blinded measurement (positive parenting SMD 0.63, negative parenting 0.43, conduct problems 0.31 in Daley 2014). Its short-term effect on core ADHD symptoms is seen by unblinded raters (SMD about 0.35 to 0.40) and is close to zero when raters are probably blinded (0.02, 95% CI -0.30 to 0.34); at about five months' follow-up Doffer 2023 reports small between-group effects on ADHD symptoms (SMD 0.21) and behaviour problems (0.16) with no masked/unmasked difference, with caveats the authors state themselves. Component analyses point to antecedent techniques and reinforcement as the active ingredients, and to heavier psychoeducation as associated with worse outcomes. (Verified 14 November 2026; see the bullets for what was read.)

### Cited Findings

Pooled effects, by who rated the outcome

- Sonuga-Barke EJS et al., 2013, *American Journal of Psychiatry* 170(3):275-289. Meta-analyses of 54 RCTs of dietary and psychological treatments. With raters closest to the therapeutic setting, psychological treatments gave SMDs of 0.40 to 0.64, all significant; the authors concluded that "better evidence for efficacy from blinded assessments is required for behavioral interventions, neurofeedback, cognitive training, and restricted elimination diets before they can be supported as treatments for core ADHD symptoms". - For behavioural interventions specifically: probably blinded ADHD symptoms SMD 0.02 (95% CI -0.30 to 0.34). **[V, full text of Daley 2014]**, which states that because no trial added since 2013 had a probably blinded measure, "the prior finding of no treatment effect for ADHD remained unchanged (overall SMD 0.02, 95% CI -0.30 to 0.34)". Which components do the work

- Dekkers TJ et al., 2022, *JAACAP*, PMID 34224837. Meta-regression of 29 studies, 138 effect sizes, N = 2,345, coding the dosage of 39 behavioural techniques from intervention manuals. Parent training had "robust small- to medium-sized positive effects on all parental outcomes". Higher dosage of techniques that manipulate antecedents was associated with better parenting sense of competence and parental mental health; higher dosage of reinforcement techniques with larger decreases in negative parenting; higher dosage of psychoeducation was negatively related to parental outcomes. The authors call the component findings exploratory; techniques were not tested in isolation. The abstract adds that the effects on parental outcomes held "both for unblinded and probably blinded measures". **[V, abstract]** — [PubMed 34224837](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34224837/). *JAACAP* 61(4):478-494; DOI 10.1016/j.jaac.2021.06.015 confirmed; authors Dekkers, Hornstra, van der Oord, Luman, Hoekstra, Groenman, van den Hoofdakker. Numeric SMDs and regression coefficients are in the full text, which could not be opened.
- The companion meta-regression on **child** outcomes: Hornstra R, Groenman AP, van der Oord S, Luman M, Dekkers TJ, van der Veen-Mulders L, Hoekstra PJ, van den Hoofdakker BJ, 2023, *Child and Adolescent Mental Health* 28(2):258-268, DOI 10.1111/camh.12561, PMID 35417075. 32 RCTs (N = 2,594 children) of parent training, teacher training or both. Higher dosage of psychoeducation for parents was associated with smaller effects on behavioural problems and, for parent training only, smaller effects on ADHD symptoms; higher dosage of negative consequences with larger effects on behavioural problems; individual rather than group training with larger effects on ADHD and hyperactivity-impulsivity symptoms. **[V, full text]** — [PubMed 35417075](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35417075/); [UvA repository PDF](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/69682681/2022_Hornstra_Groenman_van_der_Oord_Luman_Dekkers_van_der_Veen_Mulders_Hoekstra_van_den_Hoofdakker_Child_Adolescent_Mental_Health.pdf). The author uncertainty in the first pass is settled: first author Hornstra.
- **How strong the negative-consequences finding is.** From Table 2 of that paper: negative consequences (percentage of sessions), behavioural problems, 21 effect sizes, coefficient .01 (95% CI .00 to .01), p < .05, where the coefficient is "the change in SMD when the moderator increases with one … percentage point". Psychoeducation for parents: -.04 per session (-.07 to -.01) and -.01 per percentage point, both p < .01. The category "negative consequences" is defined as planned ignoring, natural or logical consequences, punishment, response cost and correction, and "further analyses on which specific techniques of that category were related to effectiveness revealed no significant results". No correction for multiple comparisons was applied because "all analyses were aimed at generating hypotheses". Almost all studies were at high risk of bias on blinding, because outcomes "were reported by parents or teachers who were also subject of the treatment". The authors add that negative consequences "are only effective when used in combination with a high dosage of … positive consequences", that the coded techniques "did not include physical punishment", and that "possible negative effects of negative consequences in the long run cannot be ruled out", citing an experiment in which children with ADHD exposed to token removal made more errors and persisted less. Overall programme effects on child outcomes in this paper ranged from .48 to .64. **[V, full text]**
- Hornstra R et al., 2021, *Journal of Clinical Child & Adolescent Psychology*, DOI 10.1080/15374416.2021.1955368. Three-arm randomised microtrial, parents of 92 children aged 4 to 12 with ADHD: two sessions of antecedent-based (stimulus-control) techniques, two sessions of consequent-based (contingency-management) techniques, or waitlist. Primary outcome daily parent-rated problem behaviour. Conclusion: "Brief training of parents in antecedent- or consequent-based techniques improves problem behaviors of children with ADHD. Antecedent-based techniques appear to be especially important to target inattention." **[V, abstract and full text]** — [PubMed 34424102](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34424102/); [Amsterdam UMC repository PDF](https://pure.amsterdamumc.nl/ws/files/157516018/Which-techniques-work-in-behavioral-parent-training-for-children-with-adhd-a-randomized-controlled-microtrial.pdf). *JCCAP* 50(6):888-903, PMID 34424102. Authors Hornstra, van der Oord, Staff, Hoekstra, Oosterlaan, van der Veen-Mulders, Luman, van den Hoofdakker.
- Hornstra 2021 effect sizes, versus waitlist, on daily parent-rated problem behaviours: antecedent condition d = .56 immediately after training and d = .65 at two weeks; consequent condition improved only at two weeks, d = .53. Behaviours then stayed stable to three months within both active conditions. Inattention symptoms fell in the antecedent condition compared with the other two at both early time points; hyperactivity-impulsivity fell in both active conditions only at two weeks; ODD symptoms did not fall. No moderators identified. Arms: antecedent n = 30, consequent n = 32, waitlist n = 30. All outcomes parent-rated; the limitations section says the parents could not be masked. **[V, abstract; arm sizes and masking from full text]**
- Not ADHD-specific, and added to close a gap: Leijten P et al., 2019, *JAACAP* 58(2):180-190, PMID 30738545. Two meta-analyses of parenting programmes for disruptive child behaviour (154 trials, 398 effect sizes). Three of 26 techniques were associated with stronger effects: "positive reinforcement, praise in particular, and natural/logical consequences". - No 2024 to 2026 meta-analysis specific to behavioural parent training and blinded outcomes was found in the searches run.

### Inferences
- The sentence already in `04-family-space.md` ("these tools help the household, and they help behaviour; they are not a treatment for the attention and impulsivity themselves") matches Daley 2014, Sonuga-Barke 2013 and Doffer 2023 as retrieved here. Doffer's "no masked/unmasked difference at follow-up" is the basis for "modest effects appear at longer follow-up". After the full-text read it stands, with the size now known (ADHD symptoms SMD 0.21) and with the authors' own caveats about confounding at follow-up; "modest" is the right word and "small" would be more exact.
- Antecedent techniques are the best fit for a phone: they are structure that can be built once and shown (routines, a visual schedule, first/then, a clear single instruction). They are also the component tied to parent sense of competence, parental mental health and child inattention. Reinforcement tools (praise prompts, a parent-run chart) map to the reduction in negative parenting.
- The psychoeducation finding argues for short Library entries and against front-loading reading before a parent can use a tool. It is a dose-association in a meta-regression, not a trial of short versus long text.
- Groenman's "protective rather than ameliorative" reading suggests honest wording: these approaches are associated with things not getting worse, particularly in harder circumstances (single parents, more conduct problems), rather than with a reduction in ADHD.
- One tension to put in front of a person rather than resolve here: the 2023 child-outcome meta-regression associates *negative consequences* with larger effects on behaviour problems, while the app's reward chart is positive-only by rule. The finding is about consequences taught inside a clinician-led programme, not about removing points in an app, and changing the chart rule would touch a hard rule. Read at source, the finding is weak support for anything of the kind: a coefficient of .01 SMD per percentage point of sessions with an interval touching zero, uncorrected for multiple testing, on unblinded ratings, for a category that is mostly planned ignoring, correction and logical consequences; response cost was one of five techniques and was not significant on its own; and the authors themselves cite experimental evidence that token removal made children with ADHD less persistent. Nothing in it argues against a positive-only chart.

### Gaps
- Pooled SMDs for Dekkers 2022 and Groenman 2022 are still unread: the full texts refused automated requests (publisher, and the Nottingham, Groningen and KU Leuven repositories). Doffer 2023 and Hornstra 2021 are now filled in above.
- Sonuga-Barke 2013 was read as abstract only.
- No component-level evidence for **planned ignoring**, **time-out**, **clear instructions/commands** or **labelled praise** individually in ADHD. Hornstra 2023 tested the techniques inside its negative-consequences category one by one and found none significant. Leijten 2019 (above) singles out praise and natural or logical consequences, in disruptive behaviour generally, not in ADHD.
- The published (typeset) version of Daley 2014 was not seen; the interval for blinded conduct problems needs checking there.
- Daily report cards are covered under question 3; their place as a "component" of parent training was not separately evidenced.
- Whether Gosling 2025 rates parent training, and at what certainty, is unknown.

## 2. Digital, app-delivered and self-directed parent training (2015 to 2026)

### Takeaway
Online parent programmes show small pooled effects on child behaviour problems (g about 0.3 to 0.4 against waitlist), but ADHD-specific evidence is thin, and the largest ADHD-relevant trial (WASH, 2025, n = 431) found a blinded effect only when self-help was combined with human support. The UK STEPS app trial (OPTIMA) had finished data collection by March 2025 and no outcome paper was found as of November 2026. Self-directed formats see lower satisfaction and attrition of roughly 30 to 50 percent, higher without practitioner support.

### Cited Findings

App and web trials in ADHD or ADHD-symptom samples

- **OPTIMA / STEPS app (UK).** Two-arm RCT of the Structured E-Parenting Support app versus wait-as-usual for families on a waiting list for ADHD assessment; parents randomised 1:1; target 352; STEPS used for 3 months; follow-up at 3, 6, 9 and 12 months; primary outcome parent-rated oppositional and defiant behaviour (SNAP-IV ODD subscale at 3 months per protocol). **[V, full text of the protocol]** — [protocol, Kostyrka-Allchorne et al., *Trials* 2022, PMC9744042](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9744042/), PMID 36510236; [ISRCTN16523503](https://www.isrctn.com/ISRCTN16523503) (registry page not readable in the verification pass). The primary outcome is parent-rated, so unblinded by design.
- OPTIMA status: King's College London update of 21 March 2025 says "data collection for the trial is complete, and we expect the results shortly"; registry lists the trial as completed. **No published outcome paper was found** in standard or extended searches. — [KCL project page](https://www.kcl.ac.uk/the-optima-trial-evaluating-the-clinical-and-cost-effectiveness-of-the-steps-parenting-app). Re-checked 14 November 2026: PubMed searches by trial name, app name and first author returned the protocol, the feasibility study, the process-evaluation protocol (Hedstrom et al., *BMJ Open* 2024, PMID 38760045) and a 2026 qualitative paper on parents' experience of waiting (Hedstrom et al., *Health Expectations*, PMID 41603377), and no outcome paper; two web searches found none either. Still unpublished or not indexed.
- STEPS feasibility and usability: during the primary recruitment period 38 of 45 consenting parents (84%) agreed in principle to take part in the RCT (41 of 48, 85%, overall); 48 of 107 parents approached (44.9%) consented to the feasibility study; System Usability Scale score 94.8 (SD 4.8) out of 100. These are usability measures, not clinical outcomes. **[V, full text]** — [JMIR Pediatrics and Parenting 2023;6:e47035, PMC10520770](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10520770), PMID 37695667; [process-evaluation protocol, BMJ Open 2024](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/491032/1/e081563.full.pdf).
- **WASH (Germany).** Three-arm RCT: treatment as usual, plus web-assisted self-help, plus web-assisted self-help with telephone support; target 495, enrolled parents of 431 children aged 6 to 12 with elevated externalising behaviour. Authors' conclusion: "Parent-directed WASH is effective in reducing blinded clinician-rated externalizing symptoms, but only when combined with additional support"; one 12-month clinician-rated effect was significant only in a per-protocol subsample (p = .035). Published 2025, *Journal of Child Psychology and Psychiatry*. **[V, full text]** — [PMC12350813](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12350813/); [protocol, Döpfner et al., BMC Psychiatry 2020, DOI 10.1186/s12888-020-2481-0](https://bmcpsychiatry.biomedcentral.com/track/pdf/10.1186/s12888-020-2481-0). Now confirmed: Döpfner M, Plück J, Rosenberger KD, Klemp MT, Mühlenmeister J, Wahnke L, Hellmich M, Schürmann S, Dose C. *JCPP* 2025;66(9):1320-1332. DOI 10.1111/jcpp.14153. PMID 40062662.
- WASH figures. The intervention ran 6 months, with assessments at baseline, 3, 6 and 12 months. Primary outcome: child externalising symptoms "as rated by a clinician blinded to condition". At 6 months, intention-to-treat overall effect p = .017; self-help with telephone support versus self-help alone, p = .029, d = -0.28 (95% CI -0.54 to -0.03); with support versus treatment as usual, p = .009, d printed as "0.34 [-0.59, -0.09]" (the sign of the point estimate is inconsistent with its interval in the abstract; read it as 0.34 in favour of support). Unsupported self-help was not shown to differ from treatment as usual. At 12 months the overall effect was significant only among families who used at least 40% of the programme (p = .035). Secondary: an effect on functional impairment at 12 months and on negative parenting at 6 months (the latter per-protocol only), both for the supported arm versus treatment as usual. At baseline 54.81% of the children took medication. **[V, full text]**
- WASH attrition and use: 98 of 431 families (22.7%, calculated here) "did not provide a complete dataset for any outcome variable" between baseline and the 6-month assessment and were counted as dropouts. 84.2% of families in the two self-help arms logged in at least once by 3 months: 88.1% with support against 80.1% without (p = .008); mean logins 5.82 against 3.92. **[V, full text]**
- WASH uptake: in 276 participants, 89.4% took up the intervention. [A] — [Child and Adolescent Psychiatry and Mental Health, March 2024](https://doaj.org/article/5434e89091194ff59e65540ff5f19e2c). Not re-checked; note it differs from the 84.2% login figure in the efficacy paper, which may use a different definition.
- **Self-help New Forest Parenting Programme.** Daley D, O'Brien M, 2013, *European Child & Adolescent Psychiatry* 22(9):543-552, DOI 10.1007/s00787-013-0396-8, PMID 23463179. 43 children randomised to a 6-week **written** self-help programme or waiting list. "ADHD symptoms were reduced, and parental competence was increased"; "forty-five percent of intervention children showed clinically significant reductions in ADHD symptoms"; the programme "did not lead to improvements in parental mental health or parent-child interaction". Child symptoms were measured by questionnaire and by direct observation; the abstract does not say which measure carried the effect, gives no effect size, and measured only before and after. **[V, abstract]** — [PubMed 23463179](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23463179/). Full text not opened.
- **Triple P Online.** Franke N, Keown LJ, Sanders MR, 2020, *Journal of Attention Disorders* 24(12):1716-1726, DOI 10.1177/1087054716667598. 53 parents of 3- to 4-year-olds with hyperactive/inattentive behaviour (27 immediate, 26 delayed). Intent-to-treat: significant post-intervention improvements in **mother-rated** hyperactivity/inattention, restlessness/impulsivity, defiance/aggression and social functioning, and in parenting measures; at 6 months some effects maintained (maternal over-reactivity and verbosity, parenting satisfaction and self-efficacy, parental stress and depression). Small sample; retrospectively registered. **[V, abstract]** — [PubMed 27609783](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27609783/); [ANZCTR entry](https://anzctr.org.au/Trial/Registration/TrialReview.aspx?id=363496). PMID 27609783. Additions from the abstract: the comparator was a delayed-intervention group; child measures were completed by mothers, fathers and teachers, and the only teacher-rated improvement was in prosocial behaviour, so the ADHD-symptom effects are mother-rated; laxness and positive parenting also improved after the intervention; none of the child-behaviour effects is listed among those maintained at 6 months. No effect sizes in the abstract. A PubMed search for other Triple P Online trials in ADHD samples found none.
- **Online versus face-to-face.** DuPaul GJ, Kern L, Belk G, Custer B, Daffner M, Hatfield A, Peek D, 2018, *JCCAP* 47(sup1):S369-S383, DOI 10.1080/15374416.2017.1342544, PMID 28715272. Pilot RCT, 47 families with children aged 3 to 5 at risk for ADHD, randomised to face-to-face or online training (10 sessions each) or waitlist. Both formats had high attendance (mean 80%) and improved parent knowledge, implementation fidelity and child behaviour ("reduced restlessness and impulsivity, improved self-control, affect, and mood") against waitlist; "parents in the F2F group reported significantly higher acceptability ratings than parents in the online group". **[V, abstract]** — [PubMed 28715272](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28715272/).
- **Blended self-directed versus therapist-led.** Randomised non-inferiority pilot, 21 referred school-age children with ADHD (blended n = 11, face-to-face n = 10), *Internet Interventions* 2019, PMID 31890615: blended parent training "was not found to be non-inferior" on behaviour problems; parents in the self-directed arm dropped out earlier and were less satisfied; therapists spent significantly less time. **[V, full text]** — [PMC6926327](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6926327/). Breider S, de Bildt A, Nauta MH, Hoekstra PJ, van den Hoofdakker BJ; *Internet Interventions* 18:100262; DOI 10.1016/j.invent.2019.100262. Outcome was the Child Behavior Checklist. The paper's introduction gives completion rates for self-directed ADHD programmes from 55% (citing Franke) to 80% (citing DuPaul) [S].
- The same paper notes that only one study in the earlier meta-analyses of self-directed parenting programmes was in children with ADHD, and cites an older meta-analysis finding no significant difference between self-directed and therapist-led interventions on child behaviour. [S] — [PMC6926327](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6926327/).

Meta-analyses of online parenting programmes (not ADHD-specific)

- A 2020 meta-analysis in *Clinical Child and Family Psychology Review* (DOI 10.1007/s10567-020-00326-0): online parenting interventions reduce behaviour problems with Hedges's g = 0.40 against waitlist. **[V, abstract]** — [PubMed 32897527](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32897527/). Florean IS, Dobrean A, Păsărelu CR, Georgescu RD, Milea I; *Clinical Child and Family Psychology Review* 23(4):510-528; PMID 32897527. 15 RCTs, N = 1,668. No significant difference between online and conventionally delivered programmes (g = -0.07). Against waitlist: parenting behaviour g = 0.34, parent distress 0.30, parenting efficacy 0.41. The authors ask for outcomes to be measured "more objectively".
- Baumel A, Pawar A, Kane JM, Correll CU, 2016, *Journal of Child and Adolescent Psychopharmacology* 26(8):740-749, PMID 27286325. Seven randomised studies (n = 718) of digital parent training versus waitlist or no treatment: child behaviour effect size 0.44 (95% CI 0.21 to 0.66); parent behaviour 0.41 (0.25 to 0.57); parental confidence 0.36 (0.12 to 0.59). The child effect was 0.61 (0.40 to 0.82) in four studies of young children with clinical-range behaviour and not significant, 0.21 (-0.01 to 0.42), in three studies of older children with non-clinical symptoms. Interactive programmes did better than non-interactive ones in the younger group. **[V, abstract]** — [PubMed 27286325](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27286325/). This is a different review from the 24-RCT one in the next bullet.
- A review of 24 RCTs of digital parent training: g = .27 to .33 versus controls; effects stronger when the intervention was evidence-based, combined an interactive platform with a therapist, and was compared with an inactive control. [A] — [AMiner record](https://www.aminer.org/pub/64a50a36d68f896efaafe37f/digital-parent-training-rct-meta-analysis-and-systematic-review). Authors and year not confirmed.
- A 2025 paper in *European Child & Adolescent Psychiatry* summarises: digital parent management training, alone or with therapist support, "has small to moderate effects on child behavior"; studies disagree on whether adding therapist support to self-directed programmes changes outcomes. [S] — [Springer, s00787-025-02799-2](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00787-025-02799-2).

Dropout and engagement

- Attrition in technology-assisted parenting interventions ranged from 30% to 50% and tended to be higher without practitioner support. [S] — review cited in a study of the self-directed ParentWorks programme, *Digital Health* 2023, [DOI 10.1177/20552076231161963](https://doi.org/10.1177/20552076231161963) (found via a proxy mirror; verify at publisher). That study classed parents as non-starters, partial completers and full completers; which predictors were significant was not retrieved. Not re-checked.
- ParentWorks attrition, from the paper PubMed holds: Dadds MR et al., 2019, *JCCAP* 48(6):881-893, DOI 10.1080/15374416.2018.1485109, PMID 30067388. A free, self-directed online programme for childhood conduct problems (not ADHD-specific) offered across Australia. 2,967 parents enrolled; 1,625 never started (54.8%, calculated here); 1,124 partly completed; 218 completed the post-intervention questionnaire (7.3%, calculated here), after "a steady and consistent loss of participants through the sequence of core program modules". Non-starters "tended to have older children with fewer behavioral problems and report higher psychopathology and dysfunctional parenting". "Contrary to findings from face-to-face research, single parents had the highest completion rates." **[V, abstract]** — [PubMed 30067388](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30067388/).
- Parental ADHD symptoms predict lower between-session adherence (home practice) but not attendance: Friedman LM, Dvorsky MR, McBurnett K, Pfiffner LJ, 2020, *Journal of Abnormal Child Psychology* 48(11):1425-1437, DOI 10.1007/s10802-020-00672-1, PMID 32813210, N = 148 children aged 7 to 11 with the predominantly inattentive presentation; adherence was parent- and clinician-rated; the association held after controlling for parental anxiety and depression, child severity and sociodemographics. In-session participation, perceived understanding and attitudes were also unrelated to parental ADHD symptoms. **[V, abstract]** — [PMC7567125](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7567125).

### Inferences
- The single clearest moderator in this literature is human support, which an app with no network cannot supply. The honest position is that Adnotia offers the *tools* a parent-training programme teaches, not the programme; no retrieved trial shows that unsupported self-help tools change blinded child outcomes in ADHD.
- That bears on tier wording in the Library (a decision for a person, not for these notes): the techniques have trial evidence when taught by a clinician; this delivery has none.
- Friedman 2020 plus the attrition figures say the realistic failure is not "the parent never opens it" but "the parent opens it and the practice between uses does not happen". Tools that work on the spot, with nothing to remember to do later, sidestep the adherence step that parental ADHD most affects.
- OPTIMA is the closest published analogue to the Family space (a UK parenting app for families waiting for assessment). Its result, when it appears, should be read before any claim about app-delivered parent support is written.

### Gaps
- OPTIMA primary outcome: unpublished or not findable as of 14 November 2026, re-confirmed in the verification pass. The ISRCTN record, which may list results, could not be read.
- The self-help New Forest Parenting Programme RCT is now reported above from its abstract; its effect sizes, and whether the observed (rather than parent-rated) measure changed, need the full text.
- Attrition figures now held: 22.7% without usable outcome data at 6 months in WASH (a trial with assessments chased by researchers); 54.8% never starting and 7.3% reaching the end in ParentWorks (open enrolment, no contact). The 30% to 50% range quoted below is still [S].
- Predictors of dropout specifically in ADHD online programmes: only fragments (support, satisfaction, parental ADHD). A dedicated search was queued and not run.
- A J Clin Psychiatry review of technology-assisted parent training ([PDF](https://www.psychiatrist.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/18118_technology-assisted-parent-training.pdf)) and a 2026 JMIR paper (e103353) surfaced in results but their content was not retrieved.

## 3. Evidence for specific child-facing aids

### Takeaway
The sleep intervention, organisational skills training and school daily report cards have RCT or meta-analytic support in ADHD. Visual schedules, visual timers and first/then boards have essentially no ADHD trial evidence that these searches could find; their evidence base is single-case research in autism and one small uncontrolled HCI study in ADHD. Reward charts are supported only indirectly, as the reinforcement component of parent training. Revised 14 November 2026 after reading the papers: the sleep intervention's benefit beyond sleep is parent-reported and was not reproduced in the larger community trial; organisational skills effects are large on ratings by parents and teachers who delivered the treatment (parent-rated g = 0.83, teacher-rated 0.54 in the meta-analysis); the report card meta-analysis rests on seven studies; and the only ADHD evidence found for time aids is one 38-child RCT of a therapist-delivered package.

### Cited Findings

Routines, visual schedules, timers, first/then

- No systematic review or trial of visual schedules, first/then boards or visual timers **in ADHD** was returned by a targeted search. — search result summary; see Gaps. **Revised 14 November 2026:** PubMed searches found a little, listed in the next four bullets. None is a trial of a stand-alone visual schedule, first/then board, visual timer or reward chart used at home by families of children with ADHD; no such trial was found, and no PubMed result at all for first/then boards or home token charts in ADHD.
- Visual activity schedules in ADHD: Thomas N, Karuppali S, 2022, *Journal of the Korean Academy of Child and Adolescent Psychiatry* 33(1):2-15, PMID 35035237 (PMC8733412). A systematic review of 2010 to 2020 that found only four studies, of mixed design (two on schedule-based tasks and an iPad for classroom skills, an RCT of psychosocial treatment for the inattentive presentation, and a cross-sectional study of group size); the one RCT was rated low quality; the authors ask for "a larger number of studies". The included studies are not tests of a visual schedule in isolation. **[V, abstract]** — [PubMed 35035237](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35035237/).
- Time aids in ADHD: Wennberg B, Janeslätt G, Kjellberg A, Gustafsson PA, 2018, *European Child & Adolescent Psychiatry* 27(3):329-342, PMID 28956183 (PMC5852175). RCT, 38 children aged 9 to 15 on stable medication, randomised to time-skill training plus time-assistive devices or to an educational intervention only; 24-week follow-up. Time-processing ability, measured by a structured assessment, rose more in the intervention group (p = 0.019); parent-rated daily time management improved (p = 0.01); "according to the children, their DTM was not significantly changed". No effect sizes in the abstract. This is a therapist-delivered package of training plus devices, not a timer alone. **[V, abstract]** — [PubMed 28956183](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28956183/).
- Time aids, mixed disabilities: Janeslätt G, Kottorp A, Granlund M, 2014, *Scandinavian Journal of Occupational Therapy* 21(3):181-190, PMID 24417452. 47 children aged 6 to 11 with ADHD, autism, intellectual disability, spina bifida or cerebral palsy; waiting-list control; 10 dropped out (21.3%). Time-processing ability d = 0.81; managing one's time d = 0.68. The authors call it "preliminary evidence". ADHD results are not reported separately in the abstract. **[V, abstract]** — [PubMed 24417452](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24417452/).
- A visual timer in a classroom, not an ADHD sample: Hallez Q, Vallier V, 2025, *European Journal of Investigation in Health, Psychology and Education* 15(12), PMID 41440149. 44 children aged 7 to 9 did a timed maths assessment with and without a visible Time-Timer (counterbalanced). Lower anticipatory anxiety and fewer "inattentive and motor instability behaviors" with the timer, more so for children at higher risk of ADHD on the Conners questionnaire; no difference in maths performance; a quarter of the children checked the timer more than seven times in five minutes and some never looked at it. One session, one school task, no diagnosed sample. **[V, abstract]** — [PubMed 41440149](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41440149/).
- MOBERO: Sonne T, Müller J, Marshall P, Obel C, Grønbæk K, 2016, CHI '16, DOI 10.1145/2858036.2858157. A smartphone system for morning and bedtime routines. Two-week study with 13 children with ADHD: improved child independence and lower parent frustration, and "a 16.5% drop in core ADHD symptoms and an 8.3% improvement in sleep habits" on questionnaires. Uncontrolled, short, parent-reported. At bedtime it prompts the parent to start, then shows a 15-minute countdown. [A] — [Bristol record](https://research-information.bris.ac.uk/en/publications/changing-family-practices-with-assistive-technology-mobero-improv/); [CHI 2016 PDF](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/1485796/1/Sonne.CHI.2016.pdf). A follow-up reported fewer conflicts around routines, with recruitment and sleep tracking made harder by running over a summer holiday [S] — [arXiv survey 2005.09993](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2005.09993).
- TangiPlan: a tangible system to help children complete morning routines, aimed at executive functioning; mentioned only, no outcomes retrieved. [S] — [Marshall et al. MOBERO paper](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1517313/1/Marshall_MOBERO%20IDC%20v16.pdf).
- Antecedent techniques as a class (which is where schedules and first/then sit) are supported by Hornstra 2021 and Dekkers 2022 above; that is evidence for the class taught to parents, not for any one aid.
- In autism, "visual supports" meet evidence-based-practice criteria in the NCAEP 2020 review with 104 single-case and 2 group-design studies. [S] — [AFIRM visual supports brief, updated 2024](https://afirm-modules.fpg.unc.edu/Visual-Supports/content/assets/raScOE/Visual%20Supports%20Brief%20Packet%2C%20Sam%20%26%20AFIRM%20Team%2C%20Updated%202024.pdf). **Contradicted at source:** the journal publication of the NCAEP review lists visual supports, defined as "a visual display that supports the learner engaging in a desired behavior or skills independent of additional prompts", with 65 articles (34 from 1990 to 2011, 31 from 2012 to 2017): 3 group-design and 62 single-case. It is one of 28 practices meeting the review's criteria, and across the whole review single-case designs were 83% of articles. **[V, full text]** — Hume K, Steinbrenner JR, Odom SL, et al., 2021, *Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders* (November 2021 issue), DOI 10.1007/s10803-020-04844-2, PMID 33449225, [PMC8510990](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8510990/). The AFIRM brief was not re-opened, so where its 104 and 2 come from is unknown; use Hume's figures.
- Knight V, Sartini E, Spriggs AD, 2015, *Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders* 45(1):157-178, DOI 10.1007/s10803-014-2201-z, PMID 25081593: 31 studies of visual activity schedules in autism, 1993 to 2013, of which **16 met criteria for acceptable quality**; schedules "can be considered an EBP for individuals with ASD, especially when used in combination with systematic instructional procedures". **[V, abstract]** — [PubMed 25081593](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25081593/).

Reward charts and token systems

- Higher dosage of reinforcement techniques in parent training is associated with larger reductions in negative parenting (Dekkers 2022), and two sessions of consequent-based techniques improved daily parent-rated problem behaviour versus waitlist (Hornstra 2021; d = .53, and only at two weeks, not immediately after training). **[V, abstract]** — [PubMed 34224837](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34224837/); [PubMed 34424102](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34424102/).
- No trial of an app-delivered or home reward chart as a stand-alone intervention in ADHD was found. Re-confirmed 14 November 2026: a PubMed search for randomised home token-economy studies in children with ADHD returned nothing.

Behavioural sleep intervention

- Hiscock H, Sciberras E, et al., 2015, *BMJ* 350:h68, DOI 10.1136/bmj.h68, PMID 25646809. RCT, 244 children aged 5 to 12 with ADHD, 21 general paediatric practices in Victoria, Australia. Comparator: usual clinical care. **Parent-reported** ADHD symptoms (ADHD Rating Scale IV) fell more in intervention families at 3 months (adjusted mean difference in change -2.9, 95% CI -5.5 to -0.3, P = 0.03, effect size -0.3) and at 6 months (-3.7, -6.1 to -1.2, P = 0.004, effect size -0.4). About a half (3 months) to a third (6 months) of the effect on ADHD symptoms was mediated through improved sleep. Authors: the brief intervention "modestly improves the severity of ADHD symptoms". **[V, full text]** — [PMC4299655](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4299655/); [PubMed 25646809](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25646809/). Authors Hiscock, Sciberras, Mensah, Gerner, Efron, Khano, Oberklaid. (bmj.com was not used.)
- **Who rated what, corrected.** Parents delivered the intervention at home and were not blind. **Teacher-rated ADHD symptoms did not differ**: "Teacher reported total ADHD scores were similar between the two groups at three and six months" (3 months: -2.4, 95% CI -5.0 to 0.3, effect size -0.2, P = 0.08; 6 months: -1.7, -4.9 to 1.6, effect size -0.1, P = 0.31, with multiple imputation). What teachers did report was "greater reductions in behaviour difficulties" on the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire at both points (effect size -0.3). The first pass ran these two together. Working memory was assessed at 6 months by research staff blinded to group, and backwards digit recall was higher in intervention children. The effect size of 0.3 and the subset sizes (n = 54 at 3 months, n = 37 at 6 months) that the first pass attached to working memory belong to **actigraphy** sleep duration (10.9 and 9.9 minutes longer, both intervals crossing zero). **[V, full text]**
- Sleep outcomes in the same trial, parent-reported: moderate to severe sleep problems at 3 months 30% in the intervention group against 56% in controls (adjusted odds ratio 0.30, 95% CI 0.16 to 0.59, P < 0.001; number needed to treat 3.9) and at 6 months 34% against 46% (0.58, 0.32 to 1.0, P = 0.07; number needed to treat 7.8). Children's Sleep Habits Questionnaire effect sizes -0.8 and -0.6. Parent-reported behaviour (-0.5, -0.4), quality of life (0.7, 0.4) and daily functioning (-0.6, -0.4) improved; parental mental health did not. **[V, full text]**
- Format of that intervention: "Sleep hygiene practices and standardised behavioural strategies delivered by trained psychologists or trainee paediatricians during two fortnightly consultations and a follow-up telephone call." **[V, abstract]**
- **The translational trial did not reproduce the wider benefit.** Hiscock H, Mulraney M, Heussler H, Rinehart N, Schuster T, Grobler AC, Gold L, Bohingamu Mudiyanselage S, Hayes N, Sciberras E, 2019, *JCPP* 60(11):1230-1241, DOI 10.1111/jcpp.13083, PMID 31184382. Moderate to severe sleep problems (parent report) at 3 and 6 months: 28.0% and 35.8% against 55.4% and 60.1% (risk ratio 0.51, 95% CI 0.37 to 0.70, and 0.58, 0.45 to 0.76). "Small sustained improvements in child wellbeing"; benefits for sleep were smaller for children not taking ADHD medication and for children whose parents had depression. **[V, abstract]** — [PubMed 30654852](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30654852/). Parent-reported ADHD symptoms d = -0.3 (p < 0.001), quality of life d = 0.4, daily functioning d = -0.5, behaviour d = -0.3. Parent- and teacher-reported outcomes were collected; the abstract gives no teacher result.
- The medication moderator above is reported as a finding about who benefited; it is not a reason for the app to record or ask about a child's medication.

Organisational skills training

- Abikoff H et al., 2013, *Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology*: dual-site RCT, 158 children with ADHD, organisational skills training (n = 64), a performance-based contingency intervention without skills teaching, PATHKO (n = 61), or waitlist (n = 33). **[V, full text]** — [PMC3549033](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3549033/). *JCCP* 81(1):113-128, DOI 10.1037/a0029648, PMID 22889336. Children in 3rd to 5th grade; 20 individual clinic sessions over 10 to 12 weeks. Organisational skills training versus waitlist: parent-rated organisational skills (COSS-P) d = 2.77; teacher-rated (COSS-T) d = 1.18; both p < .0001. Skills training beat the contingency intervention only on the parent scale (d = 0.63). 60% of children in either treatment against 3% of controls no longer met the impairment criteria for entry. **Raters:** "Most post-treatment measures were obtained from parents and teachers who were treatment agents", which the authors name as a risk of rater bias. **Maintenance:** the two treated groups (not the waitlist) were followed one and four months into the next school year, with ratings from new teachers "blind to" the treatment received; teacher-rated gains were sustained; home organisation and homework gains "declined somewhat" but stayed above pre-treatment levels; there was no untreated comparison at follow-up.
- Langberg JM, Dvorsky MR, Molitor SJ, Bourchtein E, Eddy LD, Smith ZR, Oddo LE, Eadeh HM, 2018, *JCCP* 86(1):39-55, DOI 10.1037/ccp0000265, PMID 29172596: 280 middle-school students with ADHD randomised to HOPS, a second brief school-based intervention (CHIEF) or waitlist, delivered by school mental health providers in sessions averaging under 20 minutes; parent and teacher ratings before, after and at 6 months. Both beat waitlist on parent-rated homework problems and organisational skills, "and effect sizes were large"; HOPS also showed "moderate effect size improvements" on teacher-rated materials management and organised actions. The abstract gives no numeric effect sizes and no follow-up result. **[V, abstract]** — [PubMed 29172596](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29172596/). CHOP summarises HOPS as large effects by parent report and medium by teacher report. - Bikic A, Reichow B, McCauley SA, Ibrahim K, Sukhodolsky DG, 2017, *Clinical Psychology Review* 52:108-123, DOI 10.1016/j.cpr.2016.12.004, PMID 28088557: meta-analysis of 12 RCTs, 1,054 children (576 treatment, 478 control), comparing organisational skills training with parent education, treatment as usual or waitlist. Organisational skills: teacher-rated g = 0.54 (95% CI 0.17 to 0.91), parent-rated g = 0.83 (0.32 to 1.34). Inattention: teacher-rated g = 0.26 (0.01 to 0.52), parent-rated g = 0.56 (0.38 to 0.74). The year is 2017 (published online December 2016).

School daily report cards

- Group-design meta-analysis, *Journal of Attention Disorders* (online 2017, issue 2020), PMID 29135352: systematic direct observation Hedges' g = 1.05 (95% CI 0.66 to 1.44) with very high heterogeneity; teacher-rated ADHD symptoms g = 0.36 (95% CI 0.12 to 0.60); 272 participants, mean age 7.9; comorbid externalising symptoms 0.34 (95% CI -0.04 to 0.72, p = 0.08). **[V, abstract]** — [PubMed 29135352](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29135352/). Iznardo M, Rogers MA, Volpe RJ, Labelle PR, Robaey P; *J Atten Disord* 24(12):1623-1636; DOI 10.1177/1087054717734646. Read with its limits, which the abstract states: only **seven** group-design studies; only three compared randomised treatment and control groups (a fourth had one control and three treatment groups), two were pre-post in one group and one compared two intervention groups without randomisation; pooled with fixed effects; the direct-observation figure rests on two studies with I² of 93.5%. The teachers doing the rating were also running the report card. The abstract's conclusion that the cards "have a significant effect" on co-occurring externalising behaviour does not match its own interval, which crosses zero.
- Fabiano GA, Schatz NK, Merrill BM, et al., 2025, *JCCP* 93(7):484-499, DOI 10.1037/ccp0000959, PMID 40587316. RCT, 213 students from kindergarten to seventh grade with ADHD receiving special education, daily report card built on their education-plan goals and set up by study consultants, versus school as usual. The report card group "significantly reduced the frequency of classroom rule violations, and teachers rated reductions in ADHD symptoms and in functional impairment"; no difference on teacher-rated academic performance or on meeting education-plan goals. No effect sizes in the abstract. **[V, abstract]** — [PubMed 40587316](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40587316/). This is a later and larger trial than the 2010 one listed under Gaps.
- Pyle K, Fabiano GA, 2017, *Exceptional Children*, DOI 10.1177/0014402917706370: meta-analysis of single-case studies, 14 papers, 40 cases; effect sizes "generally illustrated improvement", varying by estimation method. No pooled figure in the abstract. [A] — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0014402917706370).

### Inferences
- The aids the child surface is built from (timer, schedule, first/then) are the ones with the least direct ADHD evidence. Under `02-evidence-rubric.md` that reads as "a straightforward implementation of a technique used inside a Tier A protocol", which is the Tier C definition; the current Family table groups them with routines and praise at Tier A. Whether the Library should distinguish the technique from the specific aid is a tier question, so it is for a person other than the author.
- The sleep routine has the best child-level trial behind it. **Revised after reading the paper:** the effect on ADHD symptoms is parent-reported only; teachers saw less difficult behaviour but no change in ADHD symptoms, and the larger community trial improved sleep and nothing else. The defensible claim is that a brief behavioural sleep intervention improves parent-reported sleep, repeatedly; any claim about daytime ADHD symptoms should be attributed to parents and to the first trial. What was trialled was a clinician-delivered two-session intervention, not a routine builder. This bears on the sentence in `04-family-space.md` that the sleep intervention "has trial evidence for improving both sleep and daytime functioning", and on its proposed A/B tier; both are for a person to decide.
- Daily report cards have the strongest objective outcome (direct observation) in this set, and the school runs them. The meta-analytic base is thinner than the first pass implied (seven studies, three properly randomised); the 2025 trial of 213 children adds an observed reduction in rule violations. The existing design, which explains them and helps a parent ask, fits the evidence.
- Organisational skills evidence is for taught, school-linked programmes for roughly ages 8 to 14. A checklist tool borrows the content, not the evidence.

### Gaps
- Visual timers: one classroom experiment in unselected children and one RCT of a training-plus-devices package in ADHD are now listed above. Still no trial of a visual timer on its own in children with ADHD at home.
- First/then boards and home reward charts as stand-alone aids in ADHD: no trial found in PubMed. Stated as an absence in the searches run, not as proof that none exists.
- Morning and bedtime **checklists** as such: no study found beyond MOBERO. MOBERO itself could not be re-opened (the UCL repository refused automated requests) and stays [A].
- Fabiano et al., 2010 RCT of daily report cards (*School Psychology Review*) is not indexed in PubMed and was not found; the 2025 trial by the same group is reported above. Pyle and Fabiano 2017 is likewise not in PubMed and stays [A].
- Langberg 2018 numeric effect sizes and 6-month results need the full text.

## 4. When the parent has ADHD too

### Takeaway
Parental ADHD is common in these families (on self-report scales, from about a quarter of each parent in one sample of 323 trios to 41% of mothers and 51% of fathers in a German clinical sample of 79), is associated with household chaos, inconsistent discipline and poorer home practice in parent training, and has predicted weaker child response in two uncontrolled studies. A 2026 trial (TPAC, n = 120) reports that treating the parent's ADHD before parent training produced faster, small improvements in both parent and child clinical severity (d = 0.37 and 0.33); an earlier and larger trial (Jans 2015, n = 144) found no benefit to blinded child externalising symptoms from treating the mother first. (Revised 14 November 2026.)

### Cited Findings

How common

- In a German clinical sample, ADHD was found in 41.3% of mothers and 51.0% of fathers of children with ADHD; the paper also gives 41% to 55% of families with an affected child having at least one affected parent. Single clinical sample. **[V, full text]** — [PMC4780663](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4780663). Starck M, Grünwald J, Schlarb AA, 2016, *Neuropsychiatric Disease and Treatment* 12:581-588, DOI 10.2147/NDT.S100238, PMID 27042071 (confirmed). What the figures are: parents of 79 children with ADHD; 75 mothers and 49 fathers completed two **self-report screening scales** (Wender Utah Rating Scale and ADHS-Self-Report), with no clinical interview. Most of those above threshold reported low severity (61% of mothers, 46.9% of fathers). The study's own estimate of children with at least one parent meeting criteria was 44%. The "41% to 55%" range is the paper citing two earlier studies (Takeda 2010 for 41%, Smalley for 55%, the latter in families with two affected siblings), not its own result.
- Takeda T, Stotesbery K, Power T, et al., 2010, *Journal of Pediatrics* 157(6):995-1000, PMID 20630538. 323 parent-child trios; parental status from a self-report scale (score above 21). "ADHD criteria were endorsed by 23% of fathers and 27% of mothers, and by at least one parent in 41% of the cases." **[V, abstract]** — [PubMed 20630538](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20630538/). Larger than Starck and lower: about a quarter of each parent, not about half.
- Two later papers give the range as a background statement without their own data: "25%-50% of parents of children with ADHD" (Chronis-Tuscano 2020) and "nearly half of all youth with ADHD have at least one parent who also meets criteria" (Friedman 2020). **[V, abstracts]**, but these are assertions in introductions.
- NICE NG87 recommendation 1.4.8: "Think about the needs of a parent with ADHD who also has a child with ADHD, including whether they need extra support with organisational strategies (for example, with adherence to treatment, daily school routines)." **[V, full text]** — [NICE NG87 recommendations](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng87/chapter/Recommendations). This is the guideline hook for an app that serves both.

Parenting and the household

- In 311 mothers, mothers' ADHD symptoms were positively associated with inconsistent discipline and non-supportive responses to children's negative emotions, and "these associations were mediated by home chaos"; fathers' symptoms predicted more inconsistent discipline and low involvement. Cross-sectional, self-report. **[V, full text (author manuscript)]** — [PMC2864040](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2864040). Mokrova I, O'Brien M, Calkins S, Keane S, 2010, *Parenting: Science and Practice*, DOI 10.1080/15295190903212844, PMID 20454604. 311 mothers and 149 fathers of children in middle childhood, a community sample not selected for ADHD; child ADHD symptoms were rated by teachers. For fathers, home chaos moderated the link to inconsistent discipline and mediated the link to involvement.
- A 2019 study of parents of 31 children with ADHD and 53 without, aged 7 to 13, found "significant indirect effects between children's ADHD and certain parenting dimensions through household chaos" and none for emotional climate; the authors say this "underlines the need for interventions to improve household organization skills in parents of children with ADHD". Small, cross-sectional, questionnaires and a parent speech sample. **[V, abstract]** — Wirth A, Reinelt T, Gawrilow C, Schwenck C, Freitag CM, Rauch WA, *Journal of Attention Disorders* 23(5):451-462, DOI 10.1177/1087054717692881, [PubMed 29254391](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29254391/). **Link corrected:** the first pass pointed to PMC6532773, which is a different paper (Joseph HM et al., on 29 fathers with childhood ADHD from the Pittsburgh ADHD Longitudinal Study and their preschool children).
- Park JL, Hudec KL, Johnston C, 2017, *Clinical Psychology Review* 56:25-39, DOI 10.1016/j.cpr.2017.05.003, PMID 28601690. Meta-analysis of 32 studies: parental ADHD symptoms "accounted for 2.9%, 3.2%, and 0.5% of the variance of harsh, lax, and positive parenting, respectively"; more symptoms went with less positive and more harsh and lax parenting; the associations are "similar to the associations found between other parental psychopathologies and parenting, although the associations remain relatively small". **[V, abstract]** — [PubMed 28601690](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28601690/). The phrase "small but robust" was a secondary source's; the paper's abstract says "relatively small".

Effect on parent-training outcomes

- Sonuga-Barke EJS, Daley D, Thompson M, 2002, *JAACAP*: 83 three-year-olds with ADHD and their mothers, 8-week parent training. "Children of mothers in the high-ADHD group displayed no improvement after PT", while children of mothers with medium or low ADHD symptoms improved substantially; unrelated to other aspects of maternal mental health. **[V, abstract]** — [PubMed 12049444](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12049444/). *JAACAP* 41(6):696-702, DOI 10.1097/00004583-200206000-00009. Detail: mothers were split into high, medium and low groups on a self-report adult ADHD scale; group by time F(4,60) = 3.13, p < .05; the association held in regression (beta > .30, p < .05); follow-up at 15 weeks. No comparison group without parent training; the abstract does not say who rated the children. Full text refused by the repository.
- Chronis-Tuscano A et al., 2011, *Journal of Abnormal Child Psychology*, DOI 10.1007/s10802-011-9518-2: in mothers of 6- to 10-year-olds in brief behavioural parent training, maternal ADHD symptoms predicted post-treatment child disruptive behaviour controlling for baseline, and the relation "was mediated by change in observed maternal negative parenting". Abbreviated programme; authors call for replication. **[V, abstract]** — [PMC3715311](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3715311). PMID 21537894; *J Abnorm Child Psychol* 39(7):1047-1057. 70 mothers. Child disruptive behaviour was measured by **maternal report**; parenting by maternal report and by observation, and it was the observed negative parenting that mediated. No control group.
- Friedman LM et al., 2020 (details in question 2): parental ADHD symptoms relate to lower between-session adherence, not to attendance. [A] — [PMC7567125](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7567125).

Does supporting the parent's own ADHD help the child

- Background stated in a 2020 paper from the same group: parent training "may be less effective when the parent has ADHD", and treating parental ADHD alone had not reliably improved parenting or child outcomes. [S] — [Chronis-Tuscano et al., *J Clin Psychiatry* 2020, "Treating ADHD in parents and their children"](https://www.psychiatrist.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/18284_treating-adhd-in-parents-and-their-children.pdf). That study randomised 35 mother-child dyads to 8 weeks of maternal stimulant treatment or behavioural parent training and measured maternal symptoms, parenting and child outcomes. Its correct title is "Acute effects of parent stimulant medication versus behavioral parent training on mothers' ADHD, parenting behavior, and at-risk children" (Chronis-Tuscano A, French W, Strickland J, et al., *J Clin Psychiatry* 2020;81(5), DOI 10.4088/JCP.19m13173, PMID 32926603). Results, **[V, abstract]** — [PubMed 32926603](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32926603/): children aged 4 to 8; only the medication arm improved mothers' self-reported core ADHD symptoms; parent training improved **observed** parenting and treating the mother alone did not; children rated much or very much improved were 6% (1 child) where the mother was treated and 35% where the family had parent training (P = .04); the authors' summary is that each "had unique effects on maternal ADHD symptoms and parenting, but modest effects on at-risk children". (The abstract prints the 35% as "n = 16", which cannot be right for an arm of about 17; the count needs the full text.)
- **The largest earlier trial was negative on the child outcome.** Jans T, Jacob C, Warnke A, et al., 2015, *JCPP* 56(12):1298-1313, DOI 10.1111/jcpp.12443, PMID 26123832. Five German university clinics; 144 mother-child dyads, both with ADHD (children's mean age 9.4); mothers randomised to group psychotherapy plus open-label medication (n = 77) or supportive counselling (n = 67) for 12 weeks, after which **all** dyads had individual parent-child training. Primary outcome: change in children's externalising symptoms at 6 months, rated by an investigator blinded to assignment. "No significant between-group differences" (adjusted mean difference 1.1, 95% CI -0.5 to 2.7, p = .1854), "although maternal psychopathology improved more" with treatment. Children improved in both groups (14.8 at baseline to 11.4 and 10.3 at 6 months). **[V, abstract]** — [PubMed 26123832](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26123832/). Added in the verification pass; the first pass did not have it.
- Lui JHL, Chronis-Tuscano A, Almirall D, Whitlock KB, French W, Stein MA, 2025, *J Clin Psychiatry* 86(1), DOI 10.4088/JCP.24m15463, PMID 40009046: pilot sequential (SMART) trial in families where mother and child both have ADHD; 35 dyads randomised at baseline and again at week 8; outcomes at week 16. The sequence of parent training first and then treatment of the mother had the most favourable child outcomes (child ADHD symptoms effect size -0.36; impairment -0.33 to -0.51). Authors say results "require replication with a fully powered SMART design" and favour combining the two for most such families. **[V, abstract]** — [PMC11947710](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11947710/). Note the order that looked best here (parent training first) is the opposite of the order TPAC then tested.
- **TPAC**, Chronis-Tuscano A et al., 2026, *Journal of Child Psychology and Psychiatry*, DOI 10.1111/jcpp.70222. Hybrid effectiveness-implementation trial by telehealth, 120 parents with DSM-5 ADHD and their 3- to 8-year-old, stimulant-naive children. Treating the parent's ADHD before parent training gave faster, small-to-moderate improvements in parent and child clinical severity and modest gains in parenting consistency and positivity. Child clinician-rated severity (CGI-S) went from 4.52 to 3.67 over nine months when the parent's ADHD was treated first, versus 4.31 to 3.96 with parent training alone; parents also reported more positive parenting and less inconsistent discipline. Parent training itself improved several parenting behaviours in both arms. [S] for the CGI-S means, which are not in the abstract — [Psychiatric News alert](https://alert.psychnews.org/treating-parental-adhd-can-help-children-with-adhd); [University of Maryland summary](https://bsos.umd.edu/featured-content/evaluating-treatment-parents-and-children-affected-adhd). (The first pass listed PMC11947710 as the trial protocol; that record is the Lui 2025 pilot above.)
- TPAC as its own abstract states it, **[V, abstract]** — [PubMed 42611696](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42611696/). Chronis-Tuscano A, Bounoua N, Danko CM, Almirall D, Lui JHL, Marschall D, Taubin D, Bui HNT, Marsh NP, Efron L, Dorfman J, Lorenzo NE, Dvorsky M, Bennett IM, Atabaki SM, Robb AS. *JCPP*, published online 18 August 2026, DOI 10.1111/jcpp.70222, PMID 42611696, NCT04240756. N = 120 parents with ADHD (25% fathers) and their 3- to 8-year-old, stimulant-naive children. Both arms received a telehealth "integrated" parent training that itself targeted both parenting and the parent's ADHD, delivered by providers in urban paediatric clinics; one arm had the parent's medication titrated first. **Comparator: the same parent training without that first step**, so there is no untreated control. Children in the combined arm "showed significantly steeper declines in Clinical Global Impressions ratings" (d = 0.37, p = .001); parents' own severity fell faster (d = 0.33); parents reported greater improvement in inconsistent discipline (d = 0.22) and positive parenting (d = 0.24). "Self-reported punitive parenting and observed parenting behaviors improved similarly across" both arms. The abstract does not say whether the clinicians giving the global ratings were blind to arm, and the two parenting effects are self-reported by parents who knew their treatment; observed parenting did not differ. No confidence intervals in the abstract. Full text could not be opened (publisher refused; no repository copy).
- Chronis-Tuscano A et al., 2013: randomised mothers of children with ADHD to an integrated parenting intervention that also targeted maternal depressive symptoms, or standard parent training. Results not retrieved. [S] — [University of Maryland copy](https://bsosdev1.umd.edu/sites/psyc.umd.edu/files/pubs/ChronisTuscano%20et%20al%202013%20IPIA.pdf).

What parents with ADHD say they need (lived experience, not trial evidence)

- Consistency is the named difficulty: "children thrive on consistency, but consistency can be tough for a parent with ADHD", with bedtimes and rules falling apart at follow-through; also lower frustration tolerance and drifting during conversations. [LE] — [ADDA, "Parenting challenges with ADHD"](https://add.org/parenting-challenges-with-adhd/); [Simply Psychology overview](https://www.simplypsychology.org/adhd-parenting.html).
- A mother who recognised her own ADHD at a parent-training course: "While all the other parents brought in neatly lined, color-coded, computer-generated charts, and bragged about all the successes they were having, my hand-written graphs were crumpled, and my son's behavior was unchanged or worse." Another mother in the same article, keeping a reward chart for her seven-year-old, "was often too busy getting her other two kids ready for bed, to catch the 'teachable moments' when he complied. At other times, she admits she forgot she was keeping the charts altogether." [LE, read on the page 14 November 2026] — [ADDitude, "Mothers with ADHD raising kids with ADHD", updated June 2020](https://www.additudemag.com/mothers-with-adhd-raising-kids-with-adhd/).
- One parent with ADHD writes that consistency is "my kryptonite" and argues that consistency in connection matters more than rigid routine. [LE] — [personal Substack](https://staceysx4.substack.com/p/consistency-is-my-kryptonite).
- Parents with ADHD asking other adults with ADHD how to follow through on parenting strategies. [LE] — [HealthUnlocked adult ADHD forum thread](https://healthunlocked.com/adult-adhd/posts/148665065/how-do-i-follow-through-looking-for-parenting-strategies).
- Advice repeated across these sources: a few simple, visible, posted routines so nobody relies on memory or verbal reminders; no perfectionism; and treatment or support for the parent's own ADHD as part of supporting the child. [LE] — [Simply Psychology](https://www.simplypsychology.org/adhd-parenting.html); [Talkspace](https://www.talkspace.com/mental-health/conditions/articles/parenting-a-child-with-adhd/).

### Inferences
- An app that serves the adult's own ADHD and the child's routines is supported by the direction of this evidence: home organisation is the mediator between parental ADHD and inconsistent discipline, and TPAC suggests the parent's own ADHD being addressed first speeds the family's gains. TPAC's arm involved the parent's own clinical treatment, which the app neither delivers nor advises on; the transferable point is only that the parent's own functioning comes first. After the verification pass this inference is weaker than it reads: TPAC's effects are small and compared two active arms, Jans 2015 found no child benefit on a blinded measure, and the Lui pilot favoured the opposite order. "The parent's own ADHD matters to how parent training goes" is supported; "address the parent first" is not settled. NICE's recommendation 1.4.8, which asks clinicians to think about organisational support for a parent with ADHD, is the firmer ground for serving both in one app.
- Design for the parent who will not be consistent. Tools that still work after a missed week, with no streak and nothing to catch up on, fit both Friedman 2020 and the lived-experience reports. This is already the app's rule on guilt mechanics; here it has an evidence-based reason as well as an ethical one.
- "Visible, posted" routines argue for the printed and handed-over forms of a schedule over anything that needs the parent to remember to open the app.
- The Family space Library could say plainly that a parent having ADHD is common and is associated with finding these techniques harder to keep up, without implying anything about a particular parent. Wording for that is a "stop and ask" item.

### Gaps
- Prevalence: the figures now held run from about a quarter of mothers and fathers (Takeda 2010, n = 323 trios) to 41% and 51% (Starck 2016, n = 79 children), all from self-report scales with no clinical interview. No meta-analytic estimate of ADHD in parents of children with ADHD was found; Uchida et al., 2021 (*J Atten Disord*, PMID 32830611) meta-analyses the reverse question, risk to the children of parents with ADHD, from four studies and gives no pooled figure in its abstract. The "eightfold risk" figure was not traced.
- TPAC: effect sizes are now in hand from the abstract. Still unread: the primary outcome as pre-specified, whether the global ratings were blinded, intervals, attrition and the stated limitations. It remains the most important single paper to read in full for this question, alongside Jans 2015, which points the other way on the child outcome.
- One ADDitude first-person piece is now quoted above; no CHADD piece was looked for. Lived-experience material here is still thin. Child Mind Institute's "When parent and child both have ADHD" (2024) surfaced as a reference only.
- No study was found testing adapted parent training for parents with ADHD (shorter sessions, reminders, simplified homework) with child outcomes.

## 5. What parents praise and criticise in family and child-routine apps

### Takeaway
Everything in this section is lived experience, review-site content or vendor description, not trial evidence. Praise is consistent: fewer arguments and less nagging, a child doing a routine without being told each step, and one parent account for several children. Complaints are just as consistent: subscription price and billing, novelty wearing off in weeks, bugs and sync failures, and hardware cost. Reward charts are reported to fail through slow rewards, fading novelty and the adults not following through.

### Cited Findings

Joon [LE]

- Price $12.99 a month or $89.99 a year; some one-star reviews cite the cost; cancelling requires the app-store subscription settings and deleting the app does not stop billing, linked to Trustpilot complaints about charges after trying to cancel. — [NeuroLaunch review](https://neurolaunch.com/joon-app-review/); [Choosing Therapy review](https://www.choosingtherapy.com/joon-app-review/).

### Inferences
- The top complaints (subscriptions, billing after cancelling, sync failures, a paid device) are things Adnotia does not have. That is worth saying on the welcome page as fact, not as comparison.
- Novelty fading at four to eight weeks is a property of game mechanics. A parent-run chart with no pet, no streak and no app-initiated reward cannot fade in that way, but it also has no pull of its own; its durability depends entirely on the parent, which is where parental ADHD bites.
- The reported fixes line up with the trial evidence on immediacy: few goals, reward close to the behaviour, easy to re-set. A chart that can be awarded in one tap at the moment, and re-themed or restarted without any record of the gap, follows from both.

- Several children under one parent holder is praised wherever it exists. Per-child profiles already do this; nothing was found for or against showing siblings side by side, and the Family space already excludes comparing children.

## 6. Adolescents, 12 to 17: handing over autonomy

### Takeaway
The best-evidenced adolescent approaches are skills-based and done with the teen rather than to them: organisational skills programmes (HOPS) and a parent-teen collaborative model with motivational interviewing (STAND). STAND worked in university trials, showed no overall advantage over usual care in a community trial of 278, and its parent-teen contracting gains were not maintained at six months. Guidelines favour training interventions for this age group over classic parent-run contingency programmes.

### Cited Findings
- AAP 2019: training interventions "focused on organization, time management, and interpersonal skills" work best for middle and high school students; studies "describe some value" in parent and teacher training in behaviour management for high school students; the guideline covers ages 4 to 18 with special attention to preschoolers and teens. [S] — [AAFP summary](https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2020/0701/p58.html); [AAP news release](https://www.aap.org/en/news-room/news-releases/aap/2019/aap-updates-guidelines-on-attention-deficit-hyperactivity-disorder-with-latest-research/).
- NICE NG87, ages 5 to 18: group-based ADHD-focused support for parents and carers, which may be as few as one or two sessions, rather than a full parent-training programme for all. Recommendation 1.5.10 **[V, full text]**, quoted in question 1 — [NICE NG87 recommendations](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng87/chapter/Recommendations).
- NICE NG87 on involving young people, **[V, full text]** — [NICE NG87 recommendations](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng87/chapter/Recommendations):
  - 1.5.3: "Regularly discuss with people with ADHD, and their family members or carers, how they want to be involved in treatment planning and decisions; such discussions should take place at intervals to take account of changes in circumstances (for example, the transition from children's to adult services) and developmental level, and should not happen only once."
  - 1.5.4: before starting any treatment, discuss it with the person and their family or carers as appropriate, "encouraging children and young people to give their own account of how they feel".
  - 1.5.5: "Ask young people and adults with ADHD if they wish a parent, partner, close friend or carer to join discussions on treatment and adherence."
  - 1.5.14: "Consider a course of cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) for young people with ADHD who have benefited from medication but whose symptoms are still causing a significant impairment in at least one domain", covering social skills with peers, problem-solving, self-control, active listening and dealing with and expressing feelings.
- STAND (Supporting Teens' Autonomy Daily): Sibley MH, Graziano PA, Kuriyan AB, Coxe S, Pelham WE, et al., 2016, *JCCP* 84(8):699-712, DOI 10.1037/ccp0000106, PMID 27077693. Randomised trial, N = 128, STAND versus treatment as usual, assessed after treatment and at 6 months; 85% completed. Significant acute effects on ADHD symptoms, organisation, time management and planning skills, homework behaviour, parent-teen contracting, implementation of home privileges, parenting stress and daily homework recording; at six months effects on ADHD symptom severity, those skills and parenting stress held, but parents' use of contracting and privileges and teens' daily homework recording and homework behaviour gains "were not maintained". **[V, full text]** — [PMC4949080](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4949080/). Effect sizes against treatment as usual, after treatment: organisation, time management and planning problems d = 1.12; ADHD symptom severity d = .81; parent use of contingent privileges d = 1.07; parenting stress d = .60. These are **parent-rated**, by parents who took part in the therapy. Grade point average, the school outcome, improved by d = .33 (d = .31 at follow-up) and the group-by-time effect was not significant, "less than a quarter of a GPA point".
- STAND community trial: Sibley MH, Graziano PA, Coxe S, Bickman L, Martin P, 2021, *JAACAP* 60(6):745-756, DOI 10.1016/j.jaac.2020.07.907, PMID 32861773 (NCT02694939). N = 278 adolescents in four community clinics and 82 therapists, both randomised to STAND or usual care; outcomes after treatment (mean 5.1 months) and at follow-up (mean 9.8 months). "Intent-to-treat (N = 278) analyses indicated no significant group x time effects." STAND did better only when the therapist was licensed (22% of the sample): parent-rated inattention d = 1.08, parent-rated academic impairment d = 1.17. Parent participation was greater with STAND (d = 0.88). Conclusion: STAND "did not outperform UC on outcome trajectories" and "requires additional adaptation for community contexts". **[V, abstract]** — [PMC7907265](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7907265/). The quotations in the first pass ("Primary outcome analyses suggested…") are not from this abstract and may be from a different STAND paper; use the wording here.
- STAND three-year follow-up: Sibley MH, Graziano PA, Coxe S, Page TF, Martin P, 2025, *JAACAP* 64(10):1165-1178, DOI 10.1016/j.jaac.2024.10.009, PMID 39442667. Mean age 16.9 at follow-up. "ITT analyses indicated no long-term group by time effects." With licensed therapists: parent-rated hyperactivity/impulsivity d = 0.39, adolescent-rated parent-teen conflict d = 0.27, parent-rated organisation, time management and planning skills d = 0.79. **[V, abstract]** — [PubMed 39442667](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39442667/); [ACAMH summary](https://www.acamh.org/blog/adhd-stand-trial/). The licensure result is a moderator analysis in a fifth of the sample.
- HOPS for middle-school students and the Bikic meta-analysis: see question 3. Parent-rated effects large, teacher-rated medium. [A]/[S] — [VCU copy](https://pass.vcu.edu/media/pass/pdfs/2017-52418-001.pdf-correctedByPAVE(1).pdf); [CHOP summary](https://chop.edu/sites/default/files/adhd-exec-8-research-based-interventions-for-otmp-child-focused.pdf).
- HCI evidence on shared tools: in a study of a smartwatch-and-phone system for children with ADHD and their parents (CHI 2023, "Unpacking the lived experiences of smartwatch mediated self and co-regulation with ADHD children"), the technology gave pervasive regulation support "but the division across devices and parent-child roles interfere with developing independence". [A] — records at [NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10540812) and [eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/86x7t804); which record is which paper was not confirmed. Participants were children, not specifically adolescents.
- One popular source says research suggests that giving children some executive control over their routines can improve ADHD symptoms and executive functioning. [S, unverified] — [Talkspace](https://www.talkspace.com/mental-health/conditions/articles/parenting-a-child-with-adhd/). The underlying study was not identified.

### Inferences
- For 12 to 17 the evidence favours a tool the teen helps set up and then runs (their own materials, homework and time plan), with the parent agreeing terms rather than issuing them. In app terms that is a routine or plan built together and then handed over in a mode that is less of a kiosk than the one for a six-year-old.
- STAND's six-month fade in contracting says agreements between parent and teen decay. Making an agreement quick to revisit, without a record of having lapsed, is the design response.
- The CHI finding warns against a split where the parent holds the controls and the teen holds a display: it preserved dependence. That is a point in favour of the open question in `04-family-space.md` about adolescents having more autonomy, and it does not settle it.
- Anything a teen records about themselves runs into the open question about a child's own data, and must not become self-report screening. A teen ticking off their own plan is tool use; a teen rating their own symptoms is not on the table.

### Gaps
- NICE NG87's wording on involving children and young people is now quoted above from NICE's own page. It concerns involvement in treatment planning and decisions; it says nothing about tools or apps.
- AAP 2019 wording on adolescent assent was not retrieved, and the AAP statements in this section were not re-checked.
- STAND effect sizes are now given above. All the significant ones are parent-rated.
- No trial of a teen-facing planning app in ADHD was found, and nothing on the age at which handing over works.

## 7. Design patterns for one app serving an adult's own tasks and their children's routines

### Takeaway
Existing products use one of three models: a parent mode behind a code on a shared phone (Brili), a parent account with a separate child experience on either device (Joon), or a dedicated locked child device managed from a parent app (Goally). Research prototypes add two warnings: splitting roles across devices can hold back independence, and families want to look at things together at a deliberate time. No product or study was found that interleaves a parent's own routine with a child's, or that pairs an adult's own ADHD tools with a child's in one app.

### Cited Findings
- **Parent mode behind a code, several children, one holder.** Brili: "All kids can be managed under one parent account accessed through a user-set 4-digit code"; parents build routines in a separate Parent Mode; the account can link to unlimited devices. [LE, vendor] — [App Store listing](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/brili-routines-visual-time-r/id952895165); [Common Sense Media review](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/app-reviews/brili-routines).
- **Parent sets up, child gets a gated experience.** Joon: the parent creates the family and assigns tasks; the child gets an account to see them and can use their own device or share the parent's phone; the game is gated on completing assigned tasks. [LE, vendor] — [App Store listing](https://apps.apple.com/app/id1482225056).
- **Dedicated child device plus parent app plus carers.** Goally: a parent-managed tablet that restricts YouTube, browsers and other app stores; a separate parent app to build routines, reminders and checklists; a "care team" app for other caregivers. [LE, vendor] — [App Store listing](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/goally-adults/id1262461227); [Marlvel profile](https://marlvel.ai/apps/goally).
- **Household model.** Cozi is organised around a shared family calendar and shared lists rather than individual accounts' data; reported weaknesses are sync failures and duplicate list items. [LE] — [Morgen comparison](https://www.morgen.so/blog-posts/digital-family-calendar). OurHome assigns chores to family members with points. [LE] — [Quicken roundup](https://www.quicken.com/blog/?p=9664).
- **Parent prompted first, then the child's timer.** MOBERO's bedtime module prompts the parent to begin and then runs a 15-minute countdown; it has separate morning and bedtime modules. [A] — [Marshall et al.](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1517313/1/Marshall_MOBERO%20IDC%20v16.pdf); [Sonne et al., CHI 2016](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/1485796/1/Sonne.CHI.2016.pdf).
- **Cues for the parent, in the moment.** ParentGuardian (Pina L et al., 2014, PervasiveHealth, DOI 10.4108/icst.pervasivehealth.2014.254958) detected parental stress from a wrist sensor and showed parent-training strategy reminders on a phone and a glanceable display. [A] — [EUDL record](https://eudl.eu/doi/10.4108/icst.pervasivehealth.2014.254958); [Microsoft Research page](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=301559). It depends on sensing and is cited for the idea of a strategy card at the moment of need, not for the sensing.
- **Split roles can impede independence.** CHI 2023 smartwatch study: see question 6. [A] — [NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10540812).
- **Families want a deliberate time to look together.** "Co-designing situated displays for family co-regulation with ADHD children" (2024; co-design sessions with families, n = 23 participants): families wanted prompts "to establish intentional family time for reviewing data together". [A] — [eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/86x7t804). Authors include Silva and Cibrian per the search summary; not confirmed.

### Inferences
- Adnotia's existing shape (per-child profiles, a parent gate, a handed-over surface) is the Brili model with a stronger wall. It is the only one of the three models that needs no account, no second device and no network, so it is the only one available under the hard rules.
- A "people" or household model, in which the adult's own routine and each child's sit side by side, is not evidenced by any product or study found, and it cuts across a written boundary: `03-scope.md` and `04-family-space.md` say the two spaces "share the kernel and nothing else". Interleaving a parent's morning with a child's would need an ADR. The evidence in question 4 (home organisation as the mediator; the parent's own functioning first) is an argument for making the adult's tools easy to reach from the Family space, which is a smaller change than merging data.
- If the two are ever shown together, the privacy line is one-directional: a child on the handed-over surface must see nothing of the adult's own record. The reward chart, schedule and first/then are the only things that cross to the child today, and they are parent-authored.
- The "intentional family time" finding suggests a parent-started "look at the chart together" moment as an alternative to any reminder. It is parent-initiated, so it does not conflict with the chart rules as written.
- From the researcher's prior knowledge, not sourced in this session: a web app cannot lock a phone. iOS Guided Access and Android app pinning are operating-system features a parent can switch on to hold a handed-over phone in one app. If the Library mentions them, the wording needs checking against Apple's and Google's current help pages.

### Gaps
- Tiimo's handling of children or multiple profiles: nothing retrieved.
- Apple Guided Access and Android app pinning: the search was queued and not run; no citation.
- "Family informatics" research (shared tracking in families) was searched for by name and nothing was returned beyond the two papers above.
- No evidence was found on interleaved parent-and-child routines, on shared routines across siblings, or on how products keep a parent's own data out of a child's view beyond a code.

## 8. Autism overlap, for a possible later extension

### Takeaway
Visual supports and schedules have a much deeper evidence base in autism (single-case research: 62 of 65 articles in the NCAEP review) than in ADHD, parent training has a large RCT for disruptive behaviour in young autistic children, and a brief behavioural sleep intervention has trial evidence on caregiver-reported sleep that is smaller at 12 months (effect size -0.4) than at 3 (-0.7). (Revised 14 November 2026.) Where recommendations *differ* between autism and ADHD could not be sourced in this session.

### Cited Findings
- Visual supports are an evidence-based practice in the NCAEP 2020 review: 104 single-case and 2 group-design studies; applicable from early childhood to young adulthood. The category is broader than schedules alone. **Contradicted at source on the counts:** Hume et al., 2021 (the journal report of the NCAEP review, [PMC8510990](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8510990/)) gives 65 articles for visual supports, 3 group-design and 62 single-case **[V, full text]**; see question 3. That visual supports is one of the review's 28 evidence-based practices is confirmed. [S] for the rest — [AFIRM brief, updated 2024](https://afirm-modules.fpg.unc.edu/Visual-Supports/content/assets/raScOE/Visual%20Supports%20Brief%20Packet%2C%20Sam%20%26%20AFIRM%20Team%2C%20Updated%202024.pdf); [California CAPTAIN training module, 2022](https://captain.ca.gov/documents/CAPTAIN%20Visual%20Supports%20Module%20for%20Regional%20Center-June%201%202022%20Update.pdf), which lists individual, group and first/then boards as the main schedule types.
- Knight, Sartini and Spriggs, 2015: review of 31 papers on visual activity schedules in autism, 1993 to 2013; the paper's own abstract says schedules "can be considered an EBP for individuals with ASD, especially when used in combination with systematic instructional procedures", and that 16 of the 31 studies met criteria for acceptable quality. **[V, abstract]** — [PubMed 25081593](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25081593/); [PDF copy](https://cbmt.franciscan.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/32/2021/07/Knight-et-al.-2015_Visual-Schedules.pdf).
- Parent-run visual schedules at home: Glover, Lloyd, Barton and Mello, 2018, a multiple-baseline study across routines in two child-mother dyads, examining independent schedule use and transitions. Two dyads only. [A] — [Vanderbilt lab record](https://lab.vanderbilt.edu/lloyd-lab/publications/effects-of-parent-implemented-visual-schedule-routines-for-african-american-children-with-asd-in-low-income-home-settings).
- Parent training: Bearss K et al., 2015, *JAMA* 313(15):1524-1533, DOI 10.1001/jama.2015.3150. 180 children aged 3 to 7 with autism and disruptive behaviour, 24 weeks of parent training (n = 89) versus parent education (n = 91), six centres. Parent-rated irritability fell about 48% versus 32% (standardised effect size 0.62; treatment effect -3.9, 95% CI -6.2 to -1.7); Home Situations Questionnaire fell 55% versus 34.2% (effect size 0.45; -0.7, 95% CI -1.1 to -0.3); neither met the prespecified minimal clinically important difference; a blinded clinician rated 68.5% versus 39.6% as improved (P < .001). **[V, abstract]** — [PubMed 25898050](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25898050/) (PMC9078140). Every figure in this bullet matches. Detail: the co-primary outcomes were **parent-rated**; irritability fell from 23.7 to 12.4 against 23.9 to 16.3; the comparator, parent education, was an active control of 12 sessions and a home visit giving information about autism "but no behavior management strategies"; the paper concludes that "the clinical significance of the improvement is unclear".
- Sleep, **corrected**. The Sleeping Sound trial in autistic children: Papadopoulos N, Sciberras E, Hiscock H, et al., 2022, *JCPP* 63(11):1423-1433, DOI 10.1111/jcpp.13590, PMID 35285017. 245 children aged 5 to 13 (intervention 123, treatment as usual 122); two 50-minute sessions and a follow-up call. Parent-reported sleep problems (Children's Sleep Habits Questionnaire) at 3 months: effect size -0.7. Small effects on other child and parent outcomes "did not remain significant when controlling for multiple comparisons". Its 12-month follow-up: Pattison E, Papadopoulos N, Fuller-Tyszkiewicz M, et al., 2024, *Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders* 54(2):442-457, DOI 10.1007/s10803-022-05809-3, PMID 36414853: at 12 months (n = 150) caregivers reported a greater reduction in sleep problems, effect size -0.4, p < .001; benefit was greater for children taking sleep medication, children of parents not in psychological distress, and children with greater autism severity. **[V, abstracts; full text of Pattison checked]** — [PubMed 35285017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35285017/); [PMC9684935](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9684935/). **What changed:** the first pass gave "g = 0.64 at 3 months to 0.25 at 12 months". Those numbers are in Pattison's introduction and describe a meta-analysis of CBT for insomnia in adults (0.64 at 3 months, 0.40 at 6, 0.25 at 12). The autism trial's own figures are -0.7 and -0.4. All outcomes are caregiver-reported.
- Token economy taught to caregivers remotely: Davis et al., multiple-baseline across two participants, for routine non-preferred activities; no child-behaviour data collected. [A] — [PMC7942799](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7942799).

### Inferences
- The child-surface aids (schedule, first/then, timer) would arrive in an autism extension with *more* direct evidence than they have for ADHD, which reverses the usual direction of borrowing. Under the rubric, tiers are assessed within the population, so an autism tier would be assigned separately and could differ.
- The sleep routine is the aid with trial evidence in both groups.
- Bearss 2015 used a blinded clinician rating and an active comparison, a stronger design than most ADHD parent-training trials, and still fell short of its own clinical-importance threshold. The same care in wording would apply.

### Gaps
- **Where recommendations differ** (for example reward- and praise-based approaches with autistic children, demand-avoidant profiles, sensory aspects of timers, literal wording on a first/then board) and NICE CG170 on behaviour that challenges: nothing was retrieved. The combined query returned no relevant sources and the follow-up was not run.
- The primary Sleeping Sound autism trial is now cited from its own abstract (above). Glover 2018 and the Davis token-economy study were not re-checked and keep their tags.

## 9. Regulatory and store-policy points specific to children

### Takeaway
For an app that collects nothing and is aimed at parents, the data-protection regimes mostly have nothing to attach to: COPPA regulates operators that collect children's personal information, and the UK Children's Code applies to services that process personal data and are likely to be accessed by children. Store rules matter only if the app is listed in a store, and there the choices are whether to enter Apple's Kids Category (probably not, for a parent-facing app) and how to declare the target audience on Google Play. US state app-store age laws are a moving target in 2026. None of this is legal advice and most of it comes from secondary sources.

### Cited Findings

Apple

- Since June 2019, Guidelines 1.3 and 5.1.4 restrict Kids Category apps: originally they "may not include third-party advertising or analytics"; later softened so that third-party analytics may be permitted in limited cases, provided the services do not collect or transmit the IDFA or any identifiable information about children. Kids Category apps may not send personally identifiable or device information to third parties. [S] — [Apple developer news, 3 June 2019](https://developer.apple.com/news/?id=06032019j); [Michael Tsai's summary of the 2019 changes](https://mjtsai.com/blog/2019/06/04/updates-to-the-app-store-review-guidelines/); [third-party reproduction of the current clause](https://conductatlas.com/platform/apple/apple-app-store-review-guidelines/kids-category-third-party-data-and-advertising-restrictions/). The live guideline text was not read.
- Apple's Declared Age Range API "remains available worldwide for users on iOS 26, iPadOS 26, and macOS 26, or later". [S] — [9to5Mac, 23 Dec 2025](https://9to5mac.com/2025/12/23/texas-judge-blocks-state-app-store-age-verification-law/).

Google Play

- Developers must declare target age groups in the Play Console; Google can review whether the declaration is accurate; imagery and terms that could be considered targeting children can affect that assessment. If children are not in the target audience the app "should not unintentionally appeal to them". Apps for a mixed audience need a neutral age screen; apps solely targeting children must not transmit the advertising identifier and certain other device identifiers. — [Google Play Families policies help article](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9893335?hl=en); [Android Developers blog, 2019](https://android-developers.googleblog.com/2019/05/building-safer-google-play-for-kids.html?hl=en).
- A Families policy expansion prohibits anonymous chat apps from targeting children, effective 26 August 2026. Not relevant to an app with no network; noted as the most recent dated change found. — [Google Play policy update](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/17122218?authuser=4).

UK Children's Code

- The Code applies to information society services that process personal data and are likely to be accessed by under-18s, "even if it's not aimed at them"; "likely" means more probable than not, with no numerical threshold; the assessment looks at appeal to children and how the service is accessed, and is continuing. — [ICO, Age appropriate design code](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/guide-to-data-protection/ico-codes-of-practice/age-appropriate-design-code); [Inside Privacy on the ICO's "likely to be accessed" guidance](https://www.insideprivacy.com/childrens-privacy/uk-ico-provides-guidance-on-when-a-service-is-likely-to-be-accessed-by-children-and-needs-to-comply-with-its-age-appropriate-design-code/); [Taylor Wessing, Feb 2024](https://www.taylorwessing.com/en/global-data-hub/2024/february---childrens-data/likely-to-be-accessed-by-children).

United States

- Amended COPPA Rule: published in the Federal Register 22 April 2025, effective 23 June 2025, compliance date 22 April 2026 (with exceptions for some safe-harbour provisions). It expands the factors for "directed to children" to include marketing or promotional materials and plans, and adds a definition of a "mixed audience" site or service. — [Federal Register notice, 90 FR 76](https://downloads.regulations.gov/FTC-2024-0003-0281/content.htm); [Hunton summary](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/ftc-publishes-final-coppa-rule-amendments); [Davis Polk update](https://www.davispolk.com/insights/client-update/ftc-prioritizes-coppa-enforcement-new-compliance-obligations-take-effect).
- In February 2026 the FTC issued a policy statement limiting enforcement where children's data is collected solely for age verification. [S] — [Davis Polk update](https://www.davispolk.com/insights/client-update/ftc-prioritizes-coppa-enforcement-new-compliance-obligations-take-effect).
- Texas App Store Accountability Act (SB 2420): enjoined by a federal district court on 23 December 2025 before its 1 January 2026 effective date; the injunction was later lifted on appeal, and in July 2026 the US Supreme Court declined to block the law, so Texas can enforce it. It requires app stores to verify users' ages and pass an age category and parental-consent status to developers, and developers to assign an age rating to each app with a description of what informed it. Similar laws are enacted in Utah, Louisiana and Alabama. [S] — [Route Fifty, July 2026](https://www.route-fifty.com/management/2026/07/us-supreme-court-declines-request-block-texas-app-age-verification-law/414615/); [9to5Mac, Dec 2025](https://9to5mac.com/2025/12/23/texas-judge-blocks-state-app-store-age-verification-law/); [Troutman privacy blog](https://www.troutmanprivacy.com/?p=30307). ### Inferences
- Adnotia is a browser app and a single file, not a store listing. Apple's and Google's rules, and the state app-store laws, apply only if it is ever wrapped and listed. Nothing here is a reason to do that.
- If it were listed: it is addressed to parents and the child surface is reached only through a parent, so declaring an adult audience and staying out of Apple's Kids Category looks like the fit, and the absence of any analytics, advertising or identifier makes the data rules moot either way. Whether a handed-over child mode changes a store's view of the audience is exactly the kind of question the stores decide case by case; it is for a qualified person.
- The amended COPPA factors now include marketing materials. Welcome-page screenshots of the child surface are marketing material. With nothing collected there is still no operator in the rule's sense, which is what `03-scope.md` already concludes; the point is only that the welcome page's wording and pictures are part of how "directed to children" is judged.
- The Children's Code findings match the desk review already in `03-scope.md`: the app processes no personal data, so the Code attaches to the distribution site, not the app. Nothing found here changes that review, and nothing here strengthens it, since the ICO's own text was not read.
