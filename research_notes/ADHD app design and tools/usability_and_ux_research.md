## 1. What do HCI, co-design and usability studies with adults with ADHD say they want from apps, and what makes them abandon apps?

### Takeaway
The adult-specific HCI literature is small, recent, qualitative and mostly at concept stage. It says task management is socially and emotionally scaffolded, that no single model of support fits, and that people who already know their ADHD want help in the moment rather than more information. I found no peer-reviewed study that measures app abandonment over time in adults with ADHD or tests "novelty wearing off"; that account currently rests on vendor blogs and community posts.

### Cited Findings
- **[ADHD-adult study]** *(abstract)* 22 semi-structured interviews with adults who self-identify as having ADHD, then speed-dating of 13 speculative AI design concepts with 20 further adults. Task management is described as "a socially and emotionally scaffolded process": participants rely on body doubling, peer accountability, adaptive tools and affective support to start and sustain action. The authors state that adults with ADHD "do not benefit from a uniform model" of support, and that existing productivity tools assume steady self-regulation and linear time. Five design implications are framed around relational accountability, time as rhythm, mood-adaptive interfaces and neurodivergent affirmation. Sample skews to introspective, tool-using adults. Chen et al., 2026, ACM, DOI 10.1145/3816978 (first author from alphaXiv listing; full author list and venue name not captured). The AI co-regulator and peer accountability concepts **need a server**. — [arXiv 2603.17258](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17258); [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3816978)
- **[ADHD-adult study, students only]** *(abstract; wants list is secondary)* Individual co-design sessions with 20 university students diagnosed with ADHD plus interviews with five ADHD intervention experts. Three design directions: cognitive scaffolding for task and self-awareness, reflective task execution, and emotional regulation to sustain engagement. A secondary summary reports participants wanted fragmented task information integrated, editable prioritisation, time calibration, work broken into a few visible steps, and user control kept; experts warned of reinforced biases and dependency. Concepts only, never tested for outcomes; sample small, young and entirely Chinese. Zhu, Yu and Luo, 2026, CHI '26, DOI 10.1145/3772318.3790697. The generative-AI parts **need a server**. — [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/full/10.1145/3772318.3790697); [arXiv 2602.09381](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09381)
- **[ADHD-adult study]** *(abstract)* Real-world analytics plus 14 interviews for a free psychoeducation app (the Irish Adult ADHD App). Over 18,000 downloads between November 2022 and October 2023; 12,000 people actively used it in a year and 6,400 returned after first use. The average user started three modules and completed two. Themes: trust in the content, requests for usability changes, and a split by experience: adults new to their ADHD benefited, while adults familiar with it "sought an app that would help them in moments of challenge". The paper's background cites earlier qualitative work naming difficult navigation and pathologising language as engagement barriers. PMID 39996148 (recalled: Seery et al., Internet Interventions, 2025; confirm). — [PMC11847728](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11847728)
- **[ADHD-adult study, neurodivergent sample]** *(secondary)* Online survey of 220 people (193 neurodivergent; ADHD and autism best represented). About 75% use body doubling, many before knowing the name; uses were household chores (86), studying (64), work (52) and self-care (35); 186 said they were more likely to finish with someone present. All self-report. Body doubling mostly runs on existing tools such as video calls. Eagle, Baltaxe-Admony and Ringland, 2024, ACM Transactions on Accessible Computing 17(3), DOI 10.1145/3689648. Doing this with another person **needs a server**. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3689648)
- **[ADHD-adult study, statement in its literature review]** *(abstract)* A 2025 VR body-doubling paper states that "no studies to date have clearly demonstrated the effectiveness of body doubling". — [arXiv 2509.12153](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.12153v1)
- **[ADHD and autism, adult study]** *(abstract)* A two-phase qualitative study of self-tracking by neurodivergent researchers and participants: "Although self-tracking is often framed as enabling self-insight, this is rarely our experience as neurodivergent individuals." It notes HCI self-tracking systems centre on behaviour change, explicit goals and routine maintenance, and that the emotions tracking surfaces are hard to support. "Chasing Shadows", 2026, arXiv 2603.22609 (authors and venue not captured). — [arXiv 2603.22609](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22609)
- **[Child or mixed-age]** *(abstract)* Interviews after a 10-week remote-monitoring pilot: 10 adolescents and adults with ADHD and 12 comparison participants. Participants with ADHD said their symptoms affected participation, found the cognitive tasks more costly, and reported more technical problems than the comparison group. Denyer et al., 2023, JMIR Formative Research 7:e44126, DOI 10.2196/44126. Remote monitoring **needs a server**. — [JMIR Formative Research](https://formative.jmir.org/2023/1/e44126)
- **[ADHD-adult study]** *(abstract)* 162 adults with ADHD or high ADHD traits wrote about criticism from family, friends and colleagues. Inattention-related behaviours drew the most criticism; criticism was linked to lower self-worth and wellbeing; some coped by avoidance; feeling understood changed how criticism landed. The search results did not confirm "shame" as a named theme. Beaton, Sirois and Milne, 2022, PLoS ONE 17(2):e0263366, DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0263366. — [PMC8856522](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8856522/)
- **[General-population study]** *(abstract)* Survey of 141 people who had stopped using a Fitbit. Half described guilt about lapsing and nearly all of those wanted to return; a related survey of 193 people found others felt relief. Design guidance: show more aggregated views when there is less data, and match the framing to how the person feels about tracking. Epstein, Kang, Pina, Fogarty and Munson, 2016, UbiComp '16, pp. 829–840 (DOI 10.1145/2971648.2971656, recalled). — [Paper PDF](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~jfogarty/publications/ubicomp2016.pdf); [UW News](https://www.washington.edu/news/2016/09/08/life-after-fitbit-appealing-to-those-who-feel-guilty-vs-free/)
- **[Opinion/secondary]** The "novelty fades, the system becomes another low-interest routine" account appears on vendor and community pages (a planner-app vendor's resource page, developer blogs, forum threads) that cite no original data. — search results for this query returned no peer-reviewed study; example vendor page: [Tiimo resource hub](https://www.tiimoapp.com/resource-hub/why-productivity-systems-fail-adhd)

### Inferences
- Capture and start friction (basis: ADHD-adult interviews plus section 4). Let a person start the thing they opened the app for with no intermediate planning step; planning is itself the impaired step (Fuermaier 2013, section 4).
- "Help in moments of challenge" (basis: ADHD-adult study, one app). Tools a person opens deliberately and uses now deserve first position over reading material for anyone past diagnosis; psychoeducation serves the newly diagnosed.
- Navigation depth (basis: guidance plus one study's background citation; no ADHD-specific measurement). Keep the path to any tool short and keep module starts and finishes small: "started three, completed two" suggests content units should each be completable in one sitting.
- Language (basis: ADHD-adult study on criticism; earlier work cited for pathologising language). Copy that frames a gap or an unfinished task as the person's failure reproduces the criticism adults with ADHD report as harmful. Neutral, descriptive wording is the defensible default.
- Self-tracking (basis: neurodivergent-led qualitative study; general-population lapse study). Do not assume a log produces insight or that the person wants a goal. After a gap, show an aggregated view of what exists rather than the hole.
- Customisation (basis: ADHD-adult interviews). "No uniform model" supports opt-in tools and a home screen built from the person's own choices; it does not by itself justify a long settings surface, which adds setup burden.
- Social scaffolding cannot be delivered by an app with no network. The on-device substitutes (a visible timer, a "start with me" countdown) are untested inferences, not findings.

### Gaps
- No longitudinal study of app abandonment in adults with ADHD was found. "App graveyard", "novelty wears off" and "setup burden" are not established by published data for this population.
- No usability study measuring onboarding length, number of steps to capture, or navigation depth with adults with ADHD was found.
- Spiel, Hornecker, Williams and Good, "ADHD and Technology Research – Investigated by Neurodivergent Readers" (CHI '22) appeared only in reference lists. From background knowledge it argues that ADHD technology research mostly targets children and aims at correcting behaviour to neurotypical expectations; not retrieved, so not used as a finding.
- The rapid review of qualitative studies on adult ADHD lived experience (recalled: Ginapp et al., 2022, Frontiers in Psychiatry; [PMC9403235](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9403235/)) does not address technology directly; its coping-strategy themes were not retrieved.
- A 13-person diary study of adults with ADHD using ChatGPT as support ([DOI 10.1145/3764687.3764713](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3764687.3764713)) exists but concerns cloud AI and was not examined.

## 2. What do systematic reviews of ADHD apps and digital interventions (2018–2026) conclude about engagement, adherence and dropout?

### Takeaway
Reviews agree that ADHD apps far outnumber the evidence behind them, that adult work is dominated by web or app CBT, psychoeducation and machine-learning diagnostics, and that usability and engagement are rarely evaluated. No review identifies design features that correlate with sustained use in adults with ADHD. The engagement numbers that exist come from individual trials and from general mental-health app data, and both show steep early drop-off.

### Cited Findings
- **[ADHD review, all ages]** *(abstract)* App-store search to May 2017 (rechecked 2019): 355 ADHD apps identified, 109 included; "very few apps contained information about their development, and none contained information about evidence for efficacy or effectiveness"; of 51 papers found, one met inclusion criteria. Păsărelu, Andersson and Dobrean, 2020, International Journal of Medical Informatics (DOI 10.1016/j.ijmedinf.2020.104133, recalled). — [PubMed 32283479](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32283479)
- **[ADHD review, adults]** *(abstract)* Scoping review to December 2025: 133 studies of technologies built for adults with ADHD. 63 "treat a specific condition" (mostly web or app cognitive therapy or psychoeducation), 36 "drive clinical management" (mostly machine-learning support for diagnosis), 19 "diagnose". Conclusions call for technologies that "support adherence by incorporating lived experience". It does not judge efficacy. Schofield et al., 2026, Frontiers in Digital Health, DOI 10.3389/fdgth.2026.1746732. — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/digital-health/articles/10.3389/fdgth.2026.1746732/full); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41808914/)
- **[ADHD review, adults]** *(abstract; preliminary)* PRISMA-based scoping review in progress, presented at an ASSETS '25 workshop. Reports a sharp recent rise in papers, that most assistive-technology research still targets children, and "a significant lack of user-centered design and usability evaluations", especially in psychotherapy, psychoeducation and cognitive training. Paper counts not captured. Tan, Jost, Gerken and Pascher, 2026, arXiv 2601.21791. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.21791)
- **[ADHD review, all ages]** *(abstract)* Systematic review to May 2024 of 14 studies of ADHD apps in children and adults; several apps monitored symptoms and enhanced cognitive function. Adult-only results and engagement conclusions were not visible. Hosseinnia, Pirzadeh, Nazari and Heidari, 2025, Frontiers in Public Health 13:1483923, DOI 10.3389/fpubh.2025.1483923. — [PMC11904913](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11904913/)
- **[ADHD review, all ages]** *(abstract)* A decade of ADHD apps reviewed; 14.8% aided clinical diagnosis. Hernández-Capistrán et al., 2025, Behaviour and Information Technology 44(16):4037–4064. — [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/tbitxx/v44y2025i16p4037-4064.html)
- **[ADHD-adult study]** *(abstract)* Seven-week open feasibility study of a commercial CBT app with 240 adults recruited online: usability rated favourably; median use 3.86 times a week; assessments completed by 114 at 2 weeks, 97 at 4 weeks and 95 at 7 weeks. Uncontrolled; two authors co-founded the company. Knouse et al., 2022, PLOS Digital Health. — [PMC9931323](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9931323/)
- **[ADHD-adult study]** *(secondary; accounts conflict)* RCT of group psychoeducation supported by a smartphone app (n = 30) or paper brochures (n = 30): 43 completed (24 app, 19 brochure); the app group completed more homework (P < .001). One summary reports greater symptom improvement with the app (33.4% against 17.3%, P = .019); a later dissertation summary describes similar efficacy. Selaskowski et al., 2022, Psychiatry Research. — [Bonn repository](https://bonndoc.ulb.uni-bonn.de/xmlui/handle/20.500.11811/11599); [MDedge summary](https://www.mdedge.com/psychiatry/article/265590/adhd/adult-adhd-6-studies-nonpharmacologic-interventions/page/0/3)
- **[ADHD-adult study]** *(abstract)* A self-guided internet intervention paper calls poor adherence "a common challenge" and cites an earlier study in which 77% did not complete the intervention (which study was not captured). Nordby et al., 2022, Frontiers in Digital Health 4:821031, DOI 10.3389/fdgth.2022.821031. — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/digital-health/articles/10.3389/fdgth.2022.821031/full)
- **[General-population study]** *(abstract)* 93 Android mental-health apps with at least 10,000 installs: median 15-day retention 3.9% (IQR 10.3%) and 30-day retention 3.3% (IQR 6.2%); median daily open rate 4.0%. Day-30 retention was higher for tracker apps (median 6.1%), mindfulness apps (4.7%) and peer-support apps (8.9%, n = 2) than for breathing apps (0.0%). Baumel, Muench, Edan and Kane, 2019, Journal of Medical Internet Research 21(9):e14567, DOI 10.2196/14567. — [PMC6785720](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6785720)
- **[General-population review]** *(abstract)* Scoping review of 18 studies on when and why adults abandon lifestyle and mental-health apps. Abandonment is sharpest soon after acquisition and then slows; a median of 70% of users stopped within 100 days; mental-health apps showed 89%–92% abandonment. 22 distinct reasons, including technical and functional problems, privacy concerns, poor user experience, inadequate content, cost, changing needs, account-creation requirements and data loss. Kidman, Curtis, Watson and Maher, 2024, Journal of Medical Internet Research 26:e56897, DOI 10.2196/56897. — [JMIR](https://www.jmir.org/2024/1/e56897)
- **[Opinion/secondary]** *(abstract)* A review of digital CBT apps for ADHD in European Psychiatry says such apps may help adherence to therapy on a thin evidence base (publication type not confirmed; may be a conference abstract). — [European Psychiatry](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/european-psychiatry/article/digital-cognitive-behavioral-therapy-cbt-apps-for-adhd-a-systematic-review-of-recent-evidence/B5B9E7DAC7B382619554B612D0C6AE67)

### Inferences
- The first days decide most outcomes (basis: general-population review and panel data). Design for the first session to be useful by itself, because most people will not get a second week.
- Account creation, privacy worries and data loss are documented abandonment reasons (general population). An app with no account removes the first two; data loss is the remaining risk for a device-only app, which makes backup the engagement-relevant feature.
- A study's completion figures (Knouse: 95 of 240 at seven weeks) measure assessment completion in a recruited sample, not ordinary use, and the general figures are far lower. Do not plan around trial-level retention.
- High drop-off is not always failure. A psychoeducation module read once, or a tool used during a hard month, has done its job. This reading is mine; the reviews do not test it.

### Gaps
- No review links specific design features to sustained use in adults with ADHD. The question as asked has no evidence-based answer yet.
- Outcome papers for the Norwegian self-guided intervention RCT (Kenter et al. protocol, NCT04726813) were not retrieved, so adult module-completion rates from that trial are missing.
- Titles seen but not examined: "App fatigue in mHealth" (PLOS Digital Health, pdig.0001107), "The Super Brains app" ([PMC10417394](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10417394/)), and a real-world effectiveness study of a digital health program in adults reporting ADHD ([PMC12373820](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12373820/)).
- All retention figures here depend on usage analytics, which **need a server**. A device-only app cannot measure its own retention.

## 3. What does cognitive accessibility guidance say that applies?

### Takeaway
W3C's "Making Content Usable for People with Cognitive and Learning Disabilities" (COGA) is the most directly applicable guidance, and it is advisory: a Working Group Note, supplemental to WCAG, not required for conformance, and built on consensus and user needs rather than trials. Its patterns on focus, memory, short paths and user-controlled interruptions map closely onto ADHD. I found no ADHD-specific design guideline from a standards body; what exists is government and practitioner advice that defers to COGA.

### Cited Findings
- **[Guidance]** *(abstract)* COGA is a W3C Working Group Note, latest dated 29 April 2021. "The objectives and patterns presented here provide supplemental guidance beyond the requirements of [WCAG]. Following this guidance is not required for conformance to WCAG." — [W3C Note](https://www.w3.org/TR/2021/NOTE-coga-usable-20210429/); [current](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/)
- **[Guidance]** *(abstract)* Objective 5, "Help users focus": "Avoid using any content or elements that distract users or interrupt them. Also, consider removing content the user will find unnecessary." Its patterns include "Limit interruptions", "Make short critical paths" and "Avoid too much content". — [W3C objective page](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG2/supplemental/objectives/o5-user-focus); [2020 draft appendix](https://www.w3.org/TR/2020/WD-coga-usable-20201211/appendix_a.html)
- **[Guidance]** *(abstract)* "Limit interruptions" asks for "an easy way to control interruptions, reminders, and changes in content unless they are started by the user or involve an emergency"; options named are no interruptions, a pause, or user-selected notification settings. — [W3C pattern page](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG2/supplemental/patterns/o5p01-minimal-interruptions)
- **[Guidance]** *(abstract, from 2020 drafts)* Key content and controls should be visible without scrolling or hovering; headings and breadcrumbs help a person recover context after losing it; familiar icons, terms and patterns spare people from learning new ones; processes should not depend on remembering things such as long passwords. — [2020 working draft](https://www.w3.org/TR/2020/WD-coga-usable-20201211/Overview.html)
- **[Guidance]** *(abstract, from 2020 draft appendix)* "Provide reminders" is a COGA pattern, placed under Objective 7 (provide help and support), not under focus. Read with "Limit interruptions", the position is: reminders are a support the person sets and controls. — [2020 draft appendix](https://www.w3.org/TR/2020/WD-coga-usable-20201211/appendix_a.html)
- **[Guidance]** *(recalled; confirm names against the Note)* The eight objectives: help users understand what things are and how to use them; help users find what they need; use clear content; help users avoid mistakes and know how to correct them; help users focus; ensure processes do not rely on memory; provide help and support; support adaptation and personalisation. The Note's scope names ADHD among the conditions covered. — [W3C Note](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/)
- **[Guidance]** *(abstract, via third-party summaries)* WCAG 2.2 added nine success criteria, including 3.2.6 Consistent Help (A), 3.3.7 Redundant Entry (A: do not ask again for information already given in the same process), 3.3.8 Accessible Authentication (Minimum) (AA: no step may depend on a cognitive function test such as remembering a password unless an alternative or an assisting mechanism such as password-manager paste is available) and 2.5.8 Target Size (Minimum) (AA: 24 by 24 CSS pixels). — [WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/); [Somerset Council summary](https://playbook.somerset.gov.uk/accessibility/whats-new-in-wcag-2-2); [Accessible Web summary](https://accessibleweb.com/?p=35671)
- **[Guidance]** *(recalled; stable criteria, not re-read this session)* Older WCAG criteria that bear on attention and memory: 2.2.1 Timing Adjustable (A), 2.2.2 Pause, Stop, Hide (A: anything that moves for more than five seconds beside other content can be paused), 2.2.4 Interruptions (AAA: the person can postpone or suppress them), 2.2.6 Timeouts (AAA), 2.3.3 Animation from Interactions (AAA), 3.2.3 Consistent Navigation (AA), 3.2.4 Consistent Identification (AA), 3.3.6 Error Prevention (All) (AAA). — [WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/)
- **[Opinion/secondary]** *(abstract)* UK government practitioner guidance defers to COGA: a DWP Digital post recommends using COGA alongside WCAG; the Department for Education publishes a COGA-based guide; the UK intelligence community's design system lists ADHD-related needs such as forgetfulness and trouble organising tasks. The 2016 Home Office "dos and don'ts" posters cover autism and dyslexia; I found no ADHD poster. — [DWP Digital, 2022](https://dwpdigital.blog.gov.uk/2022/06/30/designing-accessible-services-dont-exclude-the-neurodiverse); [DfE guide](https://deliver-good-services.education.gov.uk/guides/cognitive-accessibility-guidelines); [ICDS neurodiversity page](https://design.sis.gov.uk/accessibility/needs/neurodiversity)
- **[Opinion/secondary]** *(abstract)* A University of St Andrews digital team post advises not relying on users remembering earlier steps, and using white space and concise formatting. — [St Andrews, 2025](https://digitalcommunications.wp.st-andrews.ac.uk/2025/02/12/designing-for-users-with-adhd/)

### Inferences
- Rules that follow directly from guidance (not from trials): one primary action per screen and short critical paths; nothing that moves, updates or interrupts unless the person started it; no step that depends on recalling something from an earlier screen; never ask twice for the same information; help in the same place on every screen; user control over any reminder.
- A numeric passcode is a memory test in WCAG 3.3.8's sense. Allowing paste and password-manager autofill into the passcode field is the usual way to meet the criterion without a server.
- Draft saving and undo matter more for this audience than for most: an interrupted person should find their place and their half-entered data intact (COGA objectives on mistakes and memory).

### Gaps
- No ADHD-specific interface guideline from W3C, ISO, NICE or a national ADHD body was found.
- COGA's evidence base is user-need research and expert consensus; the Note does not report controlled tests of its patterns in ADHD.
- The current COGA pattern numbering was not confirmed (w3.org was unreachable); numbers in the 2020 drafts differ from the 2021 Note.
- Nielsen Norman Group material on onboarding, notifications and dark mode was not retrieved, so none is cited.

## 4. What is known about time perception, working memory, task initiation and reward sensitivity in adult ADHD that has interface implications?

### Takeaway
The cognitive findings are real but smaller and less consistent in adults than popular accounts suggest: moderate working-memory deficits, impaired planning and time-based prospective memory, a medium preference for sooner rewards, and timing deficits that are well supported across ages but thinly studied in adults. The interface consequences (externalise memory, show time, shorten the path to starting, give feedback immediately) are reasonable inferences from these findings; none has been tested as an interface feature in adults with ADHD. I found no trial of visual timers in adults.

### Cited Findings
- **[ADHD review, all ages]** *(abstract)* Meta-analysis of 55 studies found "a broad range of timing deficits" in ADHD: small-to-medium increases in absolute error for time estimation (eight studies), and deficits in time reproduction (26 studies, 2,364 participants) with attentional and motivational components. Whether an adult-only subgroup is reported was not checked. Marx, Cortese, Koelch and Hacker, 2022, Journal of the American Academy of Child and Adolescent Psychiatry (authors and DOI 10.1016/j.jaac.2021.12.004 recalled). — [Southampton repository](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/456003)
- **[ADHD review, adults]** *(abstract)* Narrative review of a decade of adult work: "the number of studies on time perception in adult ADHD is very scarce"; some studies show distinct deficits in estimation and reproduction and others find no clear link; methods and diagnostic protocols vary. Mette, 2023, International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health 20(4):3098. — [PMC9962130](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9962130/)
- **[ADHD-adult study]** *(abstract)* Adults with ADHD were impaired in time reproduction, with unaffected first-degree relatives in between. Psychological Medicine (recalled: Pironti, Lai et al., 2016; the search summary named Lai). — [Psychological Medicine](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/psychological-medicine/article/temporal-reproduction-and-its-neuroanatomical-correlates-in-adults-with-attention-deficit-hyperactivity-disorder-and-their-unaffected-firstdegree-relatives/9AF7D4EDAA163A77450E2879537ED711)
- **[ADHD review, adults]** *(abstract; effect sizes secondary)* Meta-analysis of 38 studies: moderate working-memory deficits in adults with ADHD in both verbal and visuospatial domains (reported second-hand as 0.55 and 0.49); deficits persist into adulthood but are not found uniformly. Alderson, Kasper, Hudec and Patros, 2013, Neuropsychology. — [PubMed 23688211](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23688211/)
- **[ADHD-adult study]** *(abstract)* 45 unmedicated adults with ADHD against 45 matched controls: "a large-scale impairment" in task planning, with only negligible to small effects for plan recall, self-initiation and execution. A review cited alongside reports time-based prospective memory impaired and event-based prospective memory not. Fuermaier et al., 2013, PLoS ONE. — [PMC3590133](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3590133/)
- **[ADHD review, all ages]** *(abstract)* Meta-analysis of case-control studies: steeper monetary delay discounting in ADHD, medium effect (d = 0.43), with no significant difference by age group. Jackson and MacKillop, 2016, Biological Psychiatry: Cognitive Neuroscience and Neuroimaging. — [PubMed 27722208](https://www.pubmed.gov/27722208/)
- **[ADHD review, all ages]** *(abstract)* 37 group comparisons, 3,763 participants: small-to-medium effects for choosing small immediate over larger delayed rewards; offering real rewards in the simple choice task almost doubled the odds ratio. Adult-only estimate not seen. Marx, Hacker, Yu, Cortese and Sonuga-Barke, 2021, Journal of Attention Disorders 25(2):171–187, DOI 10.1177/1087054718772138. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/1087054718772138)
- **[General-population study, students]** *(abstract)* In 54 undergraduates, inattention was the only ADHD symptom domain correlated with general procrastination once the others were controlled. Niermann and Scheres, 2014, International Journal of Methods in Psychiatric Research. — [Paper PDF](https://www.epanlab.nl/wp-content/uploads/2017/05/Niermann-Scheres-2014.pdf)
- **[General-population study]** *(abstract)* Survey of 470 adults: more ADHD symptoms went with more procrastination and more internalising symptoms. Frontiers in Psychology, 2021 (recalled: Oguchi et al.). — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.708579/pdf)
- **[General-population study]** *(abstract)* 174 adults: ADHD symptom scores correlated with interference from task-irrelevant distractors (cartoon images), and higher perceptual load in the task removed the vulnerability. A 2021 replication found the same correlation (r = .32, N = 74) while arguing the trait claim needs independent support. Forster and Lavie, 2016, Psychological Science 27(2):203–212. — [Sussex repository](https://sro.sussex.ac.uk/id/eprint/59000/); [2021 replication, Memory & Cognition](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13421-021-01162-5)
- **[Opinion/secondary]** The only research item found on a visual timer for adult ADHD is a master's thesis: a four-week self-study by one person. Vendor pages for timer products cite no outcome data. — [Aalto University thesis record](https://aaltodoc.aalto.fi/items/68aefb43-63fc-4983-a67a-62441a5afa89)

### Inferences
All of these are inferences from cognitive findings; label them as such in any design rule.
- Time representation. Because the deficit is in estimating and reproducing durations, show elapsed and remaining time as a quantity that shrinks (a band, a disc, a bar) beside the number, and show a planned duration next to how long the same thing actually took. A visual timer belongs at "plausible", not "evidenced".
- Time-based against event-based remembering. Time-based prospective memory is the impaired kind. Tie prompts to things that happen ("when the kettle is on") or to opening the app, and avoid designs that only work if the person remembers to act at a clock time.
- Externalise working memory. Keep the current step, the previous answer and the next action on screen. Carry forward values rather than asking again. Never require holding something in mind across screens.
- Planning is the large deficit; execution is not. Offer ready-made structure (a template, a first step already written) so starting does not require planning first.
- Reward timing. Feedback should arrive at the moment of the action (a visible state change, a saved confirmation); a benefit promised next week is discounted more steeply. Real, concrete outcomes mattered more than hypothetical ones in the choice tasks.
- Irrelevant visual material costs more attention as ADHD symptoms rise (general-population correlation). Decorative images, badges and motion beside a task are the specific things to remove. An absorbing task screen protects against distraction better than a sparse one with ornament at its edge.

### Gaps
- No controlled study of analogue or visual time displays, of step-reduction, or of immediate feedback as interface features in adults with ADHD was found.
- "Time blindness" as a named construct has no agreed measurement in adults; Mette (2023) describes the adult evidence as scarce and inconsistent.
- Task initiation was not found measured directly in adults; procrastination studies are the nearest proxy and are mostly student or general-population samples.
- A meta-analysis of emotion dysregulation in adult ADHD (recalled: Beheshti et al., 2020, BMC Psychiatry) was not retrieved.

## 5. What is the evidence on gamification, streaks and rewards for people with ADHD specifically?

### Takeaway
There is no published study of streaks, points or badges in adults with ADHD, in either direction. The claim that streaks shame ADHD users and the claim that gamification suits "dopamine-seeking" brains are both unevidenced for this population. General-population research does show that a displayed broken streak reduces later engagement, most when the person blames themselves, and that gamification did not improve adherence in mental-health apps.

### Cited Findings
- **[General-population study]** *(abstract)* Seven studies: highlighting an intact streak in a behaviour log increases later engagement relative to highlighting a broken one. The effect depends on how the log represents behaviour, not on the behaviour itself; it is stronger when people attribute the break to themselves and weaker when they can "repair" the streak. Silverman and Barasch, 2023, Journal of Consumer Research 49(6):1095–1117, DOI 10.1093/jcr/ucac029. — [University of Colorado summary](https://www.colorado.edu/business/faculty-research/2023/04/19/or-track-how-broken-streaks-affect-consumer-decisions); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucac029)
- **[General-population review]** *(abstract)* Meta-analysis of mental-health apps for depression (about 50 comparisons; 25 apps with gamification, 26 without): gamification made no significant difference to effectiveness (β = −0.03, P = .38) or to adherence (β = −1.93, P = .40). Six, Byrne, Tibbett and Pericot-Valverde, 2021, JMIR Mental Health 8(11):e32199, DOI 10.2196/32199. — [JMIR Mental Health](https://mental.jmir.org/2021/11/e32199)
- **[General-population study]** *(abstract)* Half of lapsed fitness-tracker users described guilt; see Epstein et al., 2016, in section 1. — [UW News](https://www.washington.edu/news/2016/09/08/life-after-fitbit-appealing-to-those-who-feel-guilty-vs-free/)
- **[Child or mixed-age review]** *(abstract)* Reviews of games for ADHD concern children and adolescents and treat games as cognitive training or treatment, not as engagement mechanics in a self-management app. One scoping review notes the rewarding features of games may be overused; a 2025 systematic review (37 studies, 22 games) found it unclear how far products use proven therapeutic elements. — [Frontiers in Psychiatry, 2022](https://frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2022.792402/full); [Frontiers in Psychiatry, 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1605744/full); [European Child & Adolescent Psychiatry review](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00787-020-01557-w)
- **[ADHD review, all ages]** *(abstract)* Reward-timing findings (Jackson and MacKillop, 2016; Marx et al., 2021; section 4) show a preference for sooner rewards; they do not test points, badges or streaks. — [PubMed 27722208](https://www.pubmed.gov/27722208/)
- **[Opinion/secondary]** Pages asserting that streaks harm ADHD users are mostly from vendors selling streak-free products. They cite Newark and Stieglitz (2010) for a shame loop and Kwasnicka et al. (2016) for habit benefits; neither citation was verified and neither tests streaks in ADHD. One states streaks trigger "cortisol, not dopamine" with no source. — example: [Klarity blog](https://www.helloklarity.com/post/breaking-the-chain-why-streak-features-fail-adhd-users-and-how-to-design-better-alternatives)

### Inferences
- The defensible statement is narrow: displayed broken streaks demotivate in general, self-blame makes it worse, adults with ADHD report frequent criticism for inattention-related lapses (Beaton 2022), and gamification has not been shown to improve adherence. That is enough to justify leaving streaks out; it is not evidence that they harm people with ADHD specifically.
- Alternatives that give reward without a consecutive-day count, each an inference: immediate confirmation that an action was recorded; cumulative totals that cannot go down; a plain record of what happened with nothing shown for days when nothing did; aggregated views after a gap (Epstein 2016).
- If any count is shown, the general finding says the representation matters more than the behaviour: do not render a gap as a break.

### Gaps
- No adult ADHD study of streaks, points, badges, levels or virtual rewards was found, positive or negative.
- Whether novelty-based mechanics produce a faster rise and fall in ADHD than in other users is untested.
- Child findings on token economies and reward charts are parent-run behavioural interventions and do not transfer to self-administered app mechanics for adults.

## 6. What is known about notifications and reminders for ADHD users?

### Takeaway
Evidence is thin and mixed. In the one randomised test in adults with ADHD, SMS reminders did not improve module completion; non-randomised text-message programmes roughly doubled stimulant refill adherence. No study has measured habituation or alert fatigue in ADHD. In the general population, notifications raise self-reported inattention, and a prompt lifts next-day engagement only slightly.

### Cited Findings
- **[ADHD-adult study]** *(abstract)* Micro-randomised trial inside a self-guided internet intervention, 109 adults with self-reported ADHD: people who had not finished the weekly module within two days were randomised to an SMS reminder or none. "Overall results did not show an effect of SMS reminders on module completion, number of logins or practice of coping strategies"; reminders may have been linked to faster login and more time on modules. The authors raise habituation to continuous reminders as a risk to be tested, not as a finding. Nordby et al., 2022, Frontiers in Digital Health 4:821031. **Needs a server.** — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/digital-health/articles/10.3389/fdgth.2022.821031/full)
- **[ADHD-adult study]** *(secondary; figures conflict)* A clinic text-message programme (dose reminders, refill reminders and education, twice a day) was associated with higher stimulant refill adherence than usual care: summaries give 68% against 34%, 81% against 36%, and 35% rising to 70%. Comparisons were non-randomised. Biederman et al., 2019, Journal of Clinical Psychopharmacology, and a 2020 open-label pilot of 117 adults in Psychiatry Research. **Needs a server**, and concerns medication adherence. — [HCPLive summary](https://hcplive.com/view/higher-adhd-treatment-adherence-rates-linked-text-message-intervention); [CHADD article](https://chadd.org/attention-article/text-support-for-adhd/)
- **[General-population study]** *(abstract; design details recalled)* University students without ADHD reported more inattention and hyperactivity in a week with phone alerts on than in a week with alerts off. The authors say this does not show smartphones cause ADHD. Kushlev, Proulx and Dunn, 2016, CHI '16. — [Paper PDF](https://interruptions.net/literature/Kushlev-CHI16.pdf)
- **[General-population study]** *(abstract; details recalled)* A day without push notifications left participants feeling more productive and less distracted, and also more anxious about missing things and less connected. Pielot and Rello, 2017, MobileHCI '17. — [arXiv 1612.02314](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1612.02314)
- **[General-population study]** *(abstract)* Micro-randomised trial, 1,255 users of a workplace wellbeing app over 89 days: a push notification with a tailored message made engagement in the next 24 hours 3.9% more likely (risk ratio 1.039, 95% CI 1.01–1.08). The effect looked larger at weekends (not significantly) and did not change significantly over the enrolment period (P = .84). Bidargaddi et al., 2018, JMIR mHealth and uHealth 6(11):e10123. Remote push **needs a server**. — [JMIR mHealth](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2018/11/e10123/)
- **[General-population review]** *(abstract)* Adult compliance with smartphone experience-sampling prompts averages about 82% (95% CI 79.1–84.4) in a meta-analysis; a separate paper notes ADHD symptoms may predict non-response and dropout. Journal of Medical Internet Research, 2021, DOI 10.2196/17023. — [PMC7970161](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7970161); [non-adherence paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10698810/)
- **[Guidance]** *(abstract)* COGA: interruptions and reminders should be under the person's control unless they started them or there is an emergency; reminders are listed as a support pattern. — [W3C pattern page](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG2/supplemental/patterns/o5p01-minimal-interruptions)
- **[Opinion/secondary]** "Alarm fatigue" and "habituation happens faster for ADHD brains" appear on popular ADHD and product blogs with no named study. — search results returned no primary source; example: [AFFiNE blog](https://affine.pro/blog/setting-effective-reminders-adhd)

### Inferences
- An app without notifications is not missing an evidenced feature for this population. The only randomised adult ADHD result is null, the positive results are clinic-run and medication-specific, and the general effect of a prompt is a few percent.
- If reminders are ever offered, the supportable shape is: off by default, set by the person, few, tied to an event where possible (time-based remembering is the impaired kind, section 4), and silent about anything missed.
- A reminder that reports a lapse combines two things with evidence against them: an interruption and a displayed break.
- In-app prompts seen when the person opens the app need no server and no permission. They are the on-device equivalent, and untested.

### Gaps
- No study measures habituation, alert fatigue, optimal timing or wording of reminders in adults with ADHD.
- Whether locally scheduled notifications are available without a server on each platform was not researched here.
- The Biederman figures disagree across summaries and need the primary paper.

## 7. Is there credible evidence on visual design preferences (colour, contrast, density, typography, dark mode, animation) for ADHD users?

### Takeaway
Mostly unevidenced. There is no trial showing that any colour, typeface, contrast level, density or colour mode improves outcomes for adults with ADHD. What exists is one preference survey across several neurodevelopmental conditions, one very small reading study, and a general-population correlation between ADHD symptoms and distraction by irrelevant images. The strongest supported position is user control plus removal of irrelevant visual material.

### Cited Findings
- **[ADHD-adult study, mixed conditions]** *(abstract)* Survey of 204 adults with diagnosed ADHD, autism, dyslexia, dyscalculia or dyspraxia rating font style, font size, character and line spacing, title design, background colour, reward icon and instruction layout on 5-point scales. Most formatting preferences were shared across groups; "the exception to this was background colour, in which each neurodiverse group preferred a different colour". Which colours, and the ADHD-only results, were not visible. Preference, not performance. Otermans et al., 2025, Educational Media International, DOI 10.1080/09523987.2025.2544118. — [Brunel repository](https://bura.brunel.ac.uk/handle/2438/32230)
- **[ADHD-adult study, very small]** *(secondary)* Eye-tracking and pupillometry reading study of 21 participants, six with ADHD, plus five interviews. ADHD readers were fastest with a sans-serif (Roboto) at 14–16 px; 80% wanted control of font, size, brightness and background; interviewees preferred dark or warm palettes. Six university-affiliated ADHD participants and short lab passages. Llana et al., 2026 (venue not confirmed; known only from a literature-review site). — [Review page](https://a11y-paradise.onrender.com/reviews/69ed2c3f083d4508fd230b16)
- **[Opinion/secondary]** A practitioner guide states that no peer-reviewed study has tested a typeface against a clinically diagnosed ADHD sample and found a causal reading benefit; letter-spacing evidence (Zorzi et al., 2012, PNAS) is from children with dyslexia. — [Accessibility Checker guide](https://www.accessibilitychecker.org/blog/the-best-fonts-for-adhd/)
- **[Opinion/secondary]** Dark-mode benefit claims for ADHD found in search rest on anecdote or unpublished company data (one page cites about 63% of self-reporting users). — same search; no peer-reviewed source found.
- **[General-population study]** *(abstract)* ADHD symptom scores correlate with distraction by irrelevant images, and a more demanding task reduces it (Forster and Lavie, 2016; replication 2021; section 4). — [Sussex repository](https://sro.sussex.ac.uk/id/eprint/59000/)
- **[Guidance]** *(abstract and recalled)* COGA: avoid too much content, keep key controls visible without scrolling, use white space, use a consistent and familiar design, support personalisation. WCAG: 2.2.2 Pause, Stop, Hide (A) and 2.3.3 Animation from Interactions (AAA) cover motion; contrast minimums are 4.5:1 at AA and 7:1 at AAA. — [W3C COGA](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/); [WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/)

### Inferences
- Colour. No hue has evidence for an attention benefit, and preferred background colour varied by group. Treat palette as identity, meet contrast minimums, and never present a colour choice as ADHD-specific.
- Dark mode. A reasonable user preference with no ADHD evidence. If offered, it is a preference setting, and should not be described as helping focus.
- Typography. Use a legible platform sans at ordinary body sizes and respect the operating system's text-size setting. The one ADHD reading result (sans, 14–16 px) comes from six people and should not be cited as a rule.
- Density. The supported claim is about irrelevance, not emptiness: remove decoration, badges and motion that are not the task. One thing per screen follows from guidance, not from ADHD trials.
- Motion. No ADHD-specific study. Guidance supports no auto-playing or looping motion and honouring reduced-motion settings; the distractibility correlation makes decorative motion the first thing to cut.

### Gaps
- No controlled study of colour, contrast, dark mode, density or animation on task performance or sustained use in adults with ADHD was found.
- The Otermans survey's ADHD-only preferences and the Llana study's venue and full results need the primary papers.
- No study was found on icon-only against labelled navigation, or on the number of items per screen, for this population.
